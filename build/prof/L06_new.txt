[s2] V  Propositions and truth tables
  img: fig:truth
  • Proposition: a statement that is true or false
  • AND ∧, OR ∨, NOT ¬, XOR ⊕
  • Truth table: list every combination
  • p → q is false only when p is true and q is false
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: A proposition is a statement that's either true or false.
     1: It is raining.
     2: Two plus two equals four.
     3: We'll write them as letters, like p and q, and represent true as one and false as zero.
  beat1:
     0: We combine them with connectives.
     1: AND is true only when both are true.
     2: OR is true when at least one is true.
     3: NOT flips the value.
     4: And exclusive or, X O R, is true when exactly one is true.
     5: These are exactly the gates you'll build in digital logic.
  beat2:
     0: A truth table lists every combination of inputs.
     1: With two variables there are four rows.
     2: With n variables there are two to the n rows, which is why exhaustive testing of a big circuit is impossible.
  beat3:
     0: The trickiest connective is implication, p implies q.
     1: Think of it as a promise: if it rains, I'll bring an umbrella.
     2: The promise is only broken in one case: it rains and I don't bring the umbrella.
     3: If it doesn't rain, I haven't broken my promise either way.
     4: That's the highlighted row: true implies false is false, and every other row is true.

[s3] WB  Worked example: proving an equivalence with a truth table
  step0 board+: Claim:  $(p \to q) \equiv (\neg p \vee q)$ || PEN None
     0: Let's build a truth table by hand and use it to prove something.
     1: Claim: p implies q means exactly the same thing as not p, or q.
     2: Set up columns: p, q, p implies q, not p, and finally not p or q.
  step1 board+: PEN None || PEN None
     0: Row one: p true, q true.
     1: The promise is kept, so p implies q is one.
     2: Not p is zero, and zero or one is one.
     3: Row two: p true, q false.
     4: That's the broken promise: zero.
     5: Not p is zero, and zero or zero is zero.
  step2 board+: PEN None || PEN None
     0: Rows three and four: whenever p is false, the implication is automatically true.
     1: And not p is one, so not p or q is one as well.
  step3 board+: PEN None || every row matches, || so the formulas are equivalent
     0: Now compare the two highlighted columns: one, zero, one, one, and one, zero, one, one.
     1: They match in every row, so the two formulas are equivalent.
     2: That's a complete proof.
     3: Checking every row is exactly how chip verification tools prove two circuits do the same thing.

[s4] V  Equivalences and De Morgan's laws
  img: fig:demorgan
  • Contrapositive: (p → q) ≡ (¬q → ¬p)
  • Converse (q → p) is NOT equivalent
  • De Morgan: ¬(A ∧ B) ≡ ¬A ∨ ¬B
  • Simplify code and circuits
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Two formulas are equivalent if they have the same truth table.
     1: The most useful equivalence for proofs: p implies q is equivalent to not q implies not p, the contrapositive.
     2: If it rains, the ground is wet, is the same as: if the ground isn't wet, it didn't rain.
  beat1:
     0: But the converse, q implies p, is not equivalent.
     1: If the ground is wet, it rained?
     2: No, maybe the sprinklers were on.
     3: Confusing a statement with its converse is one of the most common reasoning errors, in math and in life.
     4: There's also the inverse, not p implies not q: if it didn't rain, the ground isn't wet.
     5: That fails for the same reason, sprinklers.
     6: The inverse is actually equivalent to the converse, and neither is equivalent to the original.
  beat2:
     0: De Morgan's laws tell you how to push a NOT through an AND or an OR: flip each piece and swap AND with OR.
     1: Not A and B equals not A or not B.
     2: In hardware, that means a NAND gate is the same as an OR gate with inverted inputs.
  beat3:
     0: And in code, not open parenthesis a and b equals not a or not b.
     1: Programmers use this constantly to simplify conditions, and chip design tools use it to rearrange logic into whatever gates are cheapest.

[s5] WB  Worked example: simplifying code with De Morgan
  step0 board+: if not (x > 5 and y < 3):
     0: Here's De Morgan in real code.
     1: Suppose a program has this condition: if not, open parenthesis, x is greater than five and y is less than three.
     2: It's correct, but hard to read.
     3: Let's simplify it.
  step1 board+: $\neg(A \wedge B) \equiv \neg A \vee \neg B$ || if not (x > 5) or not (y < 3):

     1: So it becomes: not x greater than five, or, not y less than three.
  step2 board+: not (x > 5)  →  x <= 5 || not (y < 3)  →  y >= 3
     0: Now negate each comparison.
     1: Not greater than five means less than or equal to five.
     2: Not less than three means greater than or equal to three.
     3: Be careful with the equals signs, because that's where most bugs hide.
  step3 board+: if x <= 5 or y >= 3: || test x = 7, y = 1:   original → False,   new → False
     0: So the final condition is: if x is at most five, or y is at least three.
     1: Test it with x equals seven and y equals one.
     2: The original: seven is greater than five and one is less than three, so the and is true, and not makes it false.
     3: The new version: seven at most five, false.
     4: One at least three, false.
     5: False or false is false.
     6: They agree.

[s6] V  Quantifiers: for all, there exists
  img: fig:quant
  • ∀x P(x): P is true for every x
  • ∃x P(x): P is true for at least one x
  • Negation flips them: ¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x)
  • "Not every test passes" = "some test fails"
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Statements about whole collections use quantifiers.
     1: For all, written as an upside down A, means the statement holds for every element.
     2: There exists, written as a backwards E, means it holds for at least one.
     3: For all integers x, x squared is at least zero: true.
     4: There exists an integer x with x squared equal to two: false.
     5: Negating a quantified statement flips the quantifier and negates the inside.
     6: Not every test passes is the same as: there exists a test that fails.
     7: To disprove a for all claim, you only need one counterexample.
     8: To prove it, you need an argument that covers every case, and that's what proofs are for.

[s7] V  Three ways to prove something
  img: fig:proofs
  • Direct: even + even = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b) → even
  • Contrapositive: n² even → n even  ⇔  n odd → n² odd
  • Contradiction: assume √2 = a/b in lowest terms … impossible
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: A proof is an argument that leaves no case uncovered.
     1: The direct method: start from what you know and march to the conclusion.
     2: Claim: the sum of two even numbers is even.
     3: Any even number is two times some integer, so write them as two a and two b.
     4: Their sum is two times a plus b, which is even.
     5: Done.
  beat1:
     0: Sometimes the contrapositive is easier.
     1: Claim: if n squared is even, then n is even.
     2: Prove the contrapositive instead: if n is odd, then n squared is odd.
     3: Odd n is two k plus one.
     4: Squaring gives four k squared plus four k plus one, which is two times something, plus one.
     5: Odd.
     6: Done.
  beat2:
     0: And proof by contradiction: assume the opposite and derive something impossible.
     1: The classic: the square root of two is irrational.
     2: Assume it equals a over b in lowest terms.
     3: Then a squared equals two b squared, so a squared is even, so a is even, using the result we just proved.
     4: Write a as two c: four c squared equals two b squared, so b squared is even, and b is even too.
     5: But then a over b wasn't in lowest terms.
     6: Contradiction.
     7: So root two can't be a fraction.

[s8] V  Mathematical induction: the domino proof
  img: fig:domino
  • Base case: true for n = 1
  • Step: if true for k, then true for k + 1
  • Example: 1 + 2 + … + n = n(n+1)/2
  • Same idea proves loops and recursion correct
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Induction proves a statement for every positive integer at once.
     1: Picture an infinite row of dominoes.
     2: First, show the first domino falls.
     3: That's the base case.
  beat1:
     0: Then show that whenever domino k falls, it knocks over domino k plus one.
     1: That's the inductive step.
     2: Together, every domino falls.
  beat2:
     0: Example: one plus two plus up to n equals n times n plus one over two.
     1: Base case: n equals one, and one equals one times two over two.
     2: Inductive step: assume it's true for k.
     3: Then the sum up to k plus one is k times k plus one over two, plus k plus one.
     4: Factor out k plus one, and you get k plus one times k plus two, over two, which is the formula for k plus one.
     5: Done, for every n.
     6: Students often worry that this is circular: aren't we assuming what we want to prove?
     7: No.
     8: We assume it for one particular k, and show that this forces it to hold for the next one.
     9: On its own, that proves nothing.
     10: Only together with the base case, the first domino, does the chain reach every n.
  beat3:
     0: Computer scientists use induction to prove programs correct.
     1: A loop invariant is a statement that's true before the loop starts, the base case, and stays true after each pass, the step.
     2: And a recursive function is correct if its base case is right and each call correctly uses smaller calls.

[s9] WB  Worked example: an induction proof you can see
  step0 board+: Claim:  $1 + 3 + 5 + \ldots + (2n-1) = n^2$ || PEN None
     0: Let's write out a complete induction proof.
     1: Claim: the sum of the first n odd numbers is n squared.
     2: One plus three plus five, up to two n minus one, equals n squared.
     3: Look at the picture: each new odd number is an L shaped layer that grows the square by one.
  step1 board+: Base, $n=1$:   $1 = 1^2$
     0: Base case: n equals one.
     1: The left side is just one.
     2: The right side is one squared, also one.
     3: True.
  step2 board+: Assume:  $1 + 3 + \ldots + (2k-1) = k^2$ || Add next:  $k^2 + (2k+1)$
     0: Inductive step: assume it's true for k, so the first k odd numbers add up to k squared.
     1: Now add the next odd number, two k plus one.
     2: The sum becomes k squared plus two k plus one.
  step3 board+: $= (k+1)^2$ || true for all n || PEN None
     0: But k squared plus two k plus one factors as k plus one, squared.
     1: That's exactly the formula for k plus one.
     2: So the statement holds for one, and each case pushes over the next, like dominoes.
     3: True for every n.
     4: And the picture agrees: one plus three plus five plus seven is sixteen, a four by four square.

[s11] V  Functions: mapping inputs to outputs
  img: fig:funcs
  • Function: every input maps to exactly one output
  • One-to-one: no two inputs share an output
  • Onto: every output is hit
  • Bijection = both → it can be inverted (e.g. encryption)
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: A function assigns each input exactly one output.
     1: Mathematicians care about two special properties.
  beat1:
     0: One to one, or injective: different inputs always give different outputs.
     1: No collisions.
  beat2:
     0: Onto, or surjective: every possible output is actually produced by some input.
  beat3:
     0: A function that's both is a bijection, a perfect pairing, and only bijections can be undone.
     1: That's why an encryption function must be a bijection: every ciphertext has to decrypt to exactly one message.
     2: A hash function, on the other hand, squeezes huge inputs into a small range of outputs, so it can't be one to one.
     3: Collisions are guaranteed, and the next slides show why.

     2: And six words chosen at random from a list of seven thousand seven hundred seventy six are even stronger, hundreds of thousands of years at this rate, while being much easier to remember.

[s13] V  Permutations and combinations
  img: fig:pascal
  • Order matters → permutations: P(n,k) = n!/(n−k)!
  • Order doesn’t → combinations: C(n,k) = n!/(k!(n−k)!)
  • Pick 2 of 6 teammates: C(6,2) = 15
  • Lottery 6 of 49: C(49,6) = 13,983,816
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: When order matters, you're counting permutations.
     1: How many ways can first, second and third place be awarded among ten runners?
     2: Ten times nine times eight, seven hundred twenty.
     3: In general, n factorial over n minus k factorial.
  beat1:
     0: When order doesn't matter, you're counting combinations: divide out the k factorial ways to arrange each group.
     1: That's n choose k.
  beat2:
     0: How many ways to pick two people from six for a project?
     1: Six choose two: six times five over two, fifteen.
     2: And look at Pascal's triangle, where each number is the sum of the two above it.
     3: Row six, position two, is fifteen.
     4: Every entry is a combination.
  beat3:
     0: And a famous one: picking six numbers out of forty nine, which is how many lotteries work.
     1: Forty nine choose six is almost fourteen million.
     2: So each ticket has about a one in fourteen million chance.
     3: Counting is also the foundation of probability, which you'll study in engineering statistics.

[s14] WB  Permutation or combination? Ask one question
  step0 board+: Rearrange the choices: a different outcome? || yes  →  permutation (order matters) || no   →  combination (order does not)
     0: The most common question in counting: is this a permutation or a combination?
     1: Ask one thing.
     2: If I rearrange my choices, do I get a different outcome?
     3: If yes, order matters, and it's a permutation.
     4: If no, it's a combination.
  step1 board+: ordered: AB BA AC CA AD DA BC CB BD DB CD DC  = 12 || PEN ('circle',) on ordered: AB BA || teams: {A,B} {A,C} {A,D} {B,C} {B,D} {C,D}  = 6 || PEN ('underline',) on teams: || $C(4,2) = \frac{P(4,2)}{2!} = \frac{12}{2} = 6$
     0: Let's see it with four people, A, B, C, D, choosing two.
     1: As an ordered list there are twelve: A B, B A, A C, C A, and so on.
     2: That's P of four, two.
     3: But as a team, A B and B A are the same team, so every team was counted twice.
     4: Twelve over two factorial is six.
     5: That's C of four, two.
  step2 board+: president + VP of 10:  $P(10,2) = 90$ || PEN ('check',) on president + VP || 2-person committee:  $C(10,2) = 45$ || PEN ('check',) on 2-person committee || 4-digit PIN (repeats OK):  $10^4$ || PEN ('circle',) on 4-digit PIN
     0: Test yourself.
     1: Picking a president and a vice president from ten people: swapping them changes the outcome, so it's P of ten, two, ninety.
     2: Picking a two person committee: swapping changes nothing, so C of ten, two, forty five.
     3: A four digit PIN: digits can repeat, so it's neither.
     4: It's the product rule, ten to the fourth.
  step3 board+: Three counting formulas || order matters, repeats allowed:   $n^k$ || order matters, no repeats:   $P(n,k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$ || order ignored, no repeats:   $C(n,k) = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}
     0: So there are really three formulas.
     1: Order matters with repeats allowed: n to the k.
     2: Order matters, no repeats: permutations.
     3: Order doesn't matter, no repeats: combinations.

[s16] V  The pigeonhole principle
  img: fig:pigeon
  • n + 1 pigeons, n holes → some hole has 2+
  • 367 people → two share a birthday (guaranteed)
  • More possible files than hash values → collisions are certain
  • Lossless compression can’t shrink every file
  (pen marks: 2 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's a deceptively simple idea.
     1: If you put more than n pigeons into n holes, at least one hole gets two pigeons.
     2: With three hundred sixty seven people in a room, two must share a birthday, because there are only three hundred sixty six possible birthdays.
     3: It sounds obvious, but it proves powerful facts.
     4: A hash function maps an unlimited number of possible files onto a fixed number of hash values, so collisions must exist.
     5: Security depends on making them hard to find, not impossible.
     6: And no compression program can shrink every file: there are fewer short files than long ones, so some inputs must get bigger.
     7: Whenever someone claims a perfect compression or a collision free hash, the pigeonhole principle says they're wrong.

[s17] V  Number theory: divisibility and the GCD
  img: fig:euclid
  • Primes: only divisible by 1 and themselves
  • GCD: the largest common divisor
  • Euclid's algorithm (≈ 300 BC): replace (a, b) with (b, a mod b)
  • gcd(252, 105) = 21 in three steps
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Number theory studies the integers.
     1: A prime is a number greater than one divisible only by one and itself: two, three, five, seven, eleven.
     2: Every integer factors uniquely into primes.
     3: Twelve is two times two times three.
  beat1:
     0: The greatest common divisor of two numbers is the largest number that divides both.
     1: It's how you reduce fractions, like the Fraction class from object oriented programming.
  beat2:
     0: Finding it by factoring is slow for big numbers.
     1: Euclid's algorithm, more than two thousand years old, is fast: the GCD of a and b equals the GCD of b and the remainder of a divided by b.
     2: Repeat until the remainder is zero.
  beat3:
     0: Let's run it on two fifty two and one oh five.
     1: Two fifty two is two times one oh five plus forty two.
     2: So find the GCD of one oh five and forty two.
     3: One oh five is two times forty two plus twenty one.
     4: Forty two is two times twenty one plus zero.
     5: The answer is twenty one.
     6: Three steps.
     7: This ancient algorithm runs inside every secure web connection today.

[s18] V  Modular arithmetic: math on a clock
  img: fig:clock
  • a mod n = remainder after dividing by n
  • 15:00 is 3 o’clock: 15 ≡ 3 (mod 12)
  • Add and multiply, then take the remainder
  • Computer integers ARE mod 2ⁿ (overflow wraps)
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Modular arithmetic is arithmetic that wraps around, like a clock.
     1: a mod n is the remainder when a is divided by n.
  beat1:
     0: Fifteen hundred hours is three o'clock, because fifteen mod twelve is three.
     1: We write fifteen is congruent to three, mod twelve.
  beat2:
     0: You can add and multiply first and take the remainder at the end, or take remainders along the way, and get the same answer.
     1: Nine plus eight is seventeen, which is five mod twelve.
     2: Seven times five is thirty five, which is eleven mod twelve.
  beat3:
     0: This isn't just a curiosity.
     1: A thirty two bit register in a C P U literally does arithmetic mod two to the thirty two, which is exactly why integers overflow and wrap around.
     2: Check digits on credit cards and book I S B Ns use modular arithmetic to catch typos.
     3: And hash tables use mod to pick a slot for each key.

[s20] V  Putting it together: RSA encryption (toy version)
  img: fig:rsa
  • Public key (n, e) — anyone can encrypt
  • Private key d — only you can decrypt
  • c = mᵉ mod n,  m = cᵈ mod n
  • Security: factoring a 600-digit n is infeasible
  (pen marks: 8 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's the payoff of number theory: R S A, one of the public key encryption systems that has secured the internet.
     1: You publish a public key so anyone can encrypt a message to you.
  beat1:
     0: But only you hold the private key that decrypts it.
     1: Let's build a toy version.
     2: Pick two primes, three and eleven.
     3: Multiply: n equals thirty three.
     4: Compute phi of n, two times ten, which is twenty.
     5: Choose e equals three.
     6: Find d so that e times d is one, mod twenty: d equals seven, since twenty one mod twenty is one.
