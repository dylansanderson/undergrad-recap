[s1] V  Simple harmonic motion: the universal oscillator
  img: fig:shm / a:quartz_watch
  • Restoring force F = −kx → x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ)
  • Spring: ω = √(k/m) · pendulum: ω = √(g/L)
  • Energy sloshes between kinetic and potential
  • Quartz crystal: 32,768 Hz = 2¹⁵ → divide 15× → 1 Hz
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Anything with a restoring force proportional to displacement, like a spring, oscillates as a perfect cosine.
     1: That's simple harmonic motion.
     2: For a spring, the angular frequency is the square root of k over m: stiffer springs vibrate faster, heavier masses slower.
     3: For a small pendulum, it's the square root of g over L.
     4: Energy constantly sloshes back and forth, potential at the turning points, kinetic in the middle, with the total constant.
  beat1:
     0: Here's where computer engineers meet it every day.
     1: Inside every watch, phone and computer is a tiny quartz crystal that vibrates like a stiff spring when driven electrically.
     2: A watch crystal is cut to vibrate at thirty two thousand seven hundred sixty eight hertz.
     3: Why that odd number?
     4: It's two to the fifteenth.
     5: Divide it by two fifteen times with a chain of flip flops, the clock divider from digital logic, and you get exactly one tick per second.

[s3] V  Damping, resonance — and the RLC connection
  img: fig:analogy
  • Friction → amplitude decays
  • Drive near the natural frequency → resonance
  • Mass–spring–damper ≡ inductor–capacitor–resistor
  • Same differential equation, same behavior
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Real oscillators lose energy to friction, so the amplitude decays.
     1: That's damping.
  beat1:
     0: Push an oscillator periodically, and if you push near its natural frequency, the amplitude grows dramatically.
     1: That's resonance, like pushing a child on a swing at just the right moments.
  beat2:
     0: Now look at this table.
     1: A mass, spring and damper obey exactly the same differential equation as an inductor, capacitor and resistor in series.
     2: Mass corresponds to inductance, spring stiffness to one over capacitance, friction to resistance.
  beat3:
     0: So the natural frequency of an L C circuit is one over the square root of L C, just as a spring's is the square root of k over m.
     1: Radios use this resonance to select one station out of thousands.
     2: Same physics, same math, different parts.

[s4] V  Waves: v = f λ
  img: fig:wave
  • A wave carries energy, not matter
  • Transverse (string, light) vs longitudinal (sound)
  • Middle A, 440 Hz in air: λ = 343/440 ≈ 78 cm
  • Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz: λ = c/f ≈ 12.5 cm
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: A wave is a disturbance that travels, carrying energy without carrying matter along.
     1: A stadium wave moves around the stadium, but each person just stands up and sits down.
  beat1:
     0: In a transverse wave, like a wave on a string or light, the wiggle is perpendicular to the direction of travel.
     1: In a longitudinal wave, like sound, the air molecules are pushed back and forth along the direction of travel.
  beat2:
     0: Every wave obeys the simplest formula in physics: speed equals frequency times wavelength.
     1: Sound travels at about three hundred forty three meters per second in air.
     2: Middle A, at four hundred forty hertz, has a wavelength of about seventy eight centimeters.
  beat3:
     0: Radio waves travel at the speed of light.
     1: Wi Fi at two point four gigahertz has a wavelength of about twelve and a half centimeters, which is why Wi Fi antennas are a few centimeters long.
     2: Antenna size is set by wavelength.

[s5] V  Superposition and standing waves
  img: fig:standing
  • Waves add: constructive + destructive interference
  • String fixed at both ends: λₙ = 2L/n
  • Harmonics f, 2f, 3f … give instruments their tone
  • Noise-cancelling headphones: add the inverse wave
  (pen marks: 8 )
  beat0:
     0: When two waves overlap, their displacements simply add.
     1: That's superposition.
     2: Crest on crest makes a bigger wave, constructive interference.
     3: Crest on trough cancels, destructive interference.
  beat1:
     0: A string fixed at both ends, like a guitar string, can only vibrate in patterns that fit: wavelengths of two L over n.
     1: These are standing waves, with fixed points called nodes.
  beat2:
     0: So a string vibrates at its fundamental frequency and at whole number multiples, called harmonics.
     1: The mix of harmonics is why a guitar and a piano playing the same note sound different.
     2: And the idea that any sound is a sum of sine waves at different frequencies is exactly Fourier analysis, which you'll use in signals and systems.
  beat3:
     0: Destructive interference is also how noise cancelling headphones work: a microphone hears the noise, and the electronics play the inverted wave, so the two cancel at your ear.

[s7] V  Sound: decibels and the Doppler effect
  img: fig:db / fig:doppler
  • β = 10 log(I/I₀) dB — a logarithmic scale
  • +10 dB = 10× intensity; +3 dB = 2×
  • Doppler: moving source → shifted frequency
  • Radar guns, weather radar, ultrasound blood flow
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Our ears handle an enormous range of intensities, so sound is measured on a logarithmic scale, in decibels.
     1: Every ten decibels is ten times the intensity.
     2: A rock concert at one hundred ten decibels is a hundred billion times more intense than the faintest sound you can hear.
     3: Engineers use decibels for signal strength and amplifier gain too: plus three decibels means double the power.
  beat1:
     0: And when a source moves, its waves bunch up in front and stretch out behind.
     1: That's the Doppler effect.
     2: A siren at one thousand hertz moving toward you at thirty meters per second sounds like about one thousand ninety six hertz, then drops to about nine hundred twenty as it passes.
     3: Police radar guns, weather radar and medical ultrasound all measure speed by measuring this frequency shift.

[s9] V  Light bends: refraction and Snell’s law
  img: a:prism / fig:snell
  • Light slows in glass: n = c / v
  • n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
  • Air → glass at 30°: bends to 19.5°
  • n depends on color → prisms split white light
  beat0:
     0: Light travels slower in glass or water than in air.
     1: The index of refraction, n, is how many times slower.
     2: For glass, about one and a half.
     3: When light enters at an angle, the change in speed bends it.
     4: And since the index depends slightly on the color, a prism bends each color by a different amount, splitting white light into a rainbow.
  beat1:
     0: Snell's law gives the angle: n one sine theta one equals n two sine theta two.
     1: From air into glass at thirty degrees, the sine of the new angle is point five divided by one point five, so the light bends to about nineteen and a half degrees, toward the perpendicular, as it slows down.

[s11] V  Total internal reflection: the internet is made of light
  img: fig:tir
  • Dense → less dense, beyond the critical angle: 100% reflection
  • sin θc = n₂/n₁ → 41.8° for glass to air
  • Optical fiber traps light for kilometers
  • Undersea cables carry most intercontinental data
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Going the other way, from glass into air, light bends away from the perpendicular.
     1: At a steep enough angle, the critical angle, it can't get out at all.
     2: Every bit of it reflects back inside.
     3: That's total internal reflection.
     4: For glass to air, the sine of the critical angle is one over one point five, about forty two degrees.
     5: An optical fiber is a thin glass core surrounded by cladding with a slightly lower index, so light bounces along the core for kilometers.
  beat1:
     0: These fibers carry the backbone of the internet.
     1: Undersea fiber cables carry the vast majority of data between continents, with lasers blinking billions of times per second.
     2: Every video you stream from another country probably traveled as light through glass, trapped by this one equation.

[s13] V  Interference and diffraction: light is a wave
  img: fig:slits
  • Two slits: bright where d sin θ = mλ
  • Fringe spacing = λL/d = 13 mm in this example
  • Diffraction limits how small you can image
  • Chipmakers use 13.5 nm extreme-UV light to print tiny features
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Shine a laser through two narrow slits, and instead of two bright lines you get a whole row of bright and dark fringes.
     1: Light from the two slits interferes: where the path lengths differ by a whole number of wavelengths, the waves add, and where they differ by half a wavelength, they cancel.
     2: This experiment, first done by Thomas Young around eighteen hundred, proved light is a wave.
  beat1:
     0: The fringe spacing is wavelength times distance to the screen, over the slit separation.
     1: With a red laser at six hundred fifty nanometers, slits a tenth of a millimeter apart, and a screen two meters away, the fringes are thirteen millimeters apart.
  beat2:
     0: Diffraction also sets a fundamental limit: you can't focus light to a spot much smaller than its wavelength.
     1: That's a huge problem for chipmakers, who need to print transistor features just a few tens of nanometers across.
  beat3:
     0: So the most advanced chip factories use extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of thirteen and a half nanometers, generated by blasting tiny droplets of tin with a laser.
     1: Wave optics directly limits how small transistors can be.

[s14] V  Relativity — and why GPS needs it
  img: a:gps_sat / fig:gps
  • Speed of light is the same for every observer
  • Moving clocks run slow: Δt = γΔt₀
  • Clocks in weaker gravity run fast
  • GPS: net +38 μs/day — uncorrected, ~10 km error per day
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: In nineteen oh five, Einstein built special relativity on one surprising fact: the speed of light is the same for every observer, no matter how they move.
     1: The consequences: moving clocks run slow, by the factor gamma.
     2: Moving objects contract.
     3: And mass and energy are equivalent, E equals m c squared.
     4: His later theory, general relativity, adds that clocks run faster where gravity is weaker.
  beat1:
     0: This isn't abstract.
     1: GPS satellites carry atomic clocks and move at about fourteen thousand kilometers per hour.
     2: Special relativity makes their clocks lose about seven microseconds per day.
     3: But they're high above Earth, in weaker gravity, so general relativity makes them gain about forty five.
     4: The net is about thirty eight microseconds per day fast.
     5: Since GPS measures distance by timing radio signals at the speed of light, thirty eight microseconds is about eleven kilometers of error, every single day, if engineers didn't correct for it.
     6: They do, by deliberately setting the satellite clocks slightly slow before launch.

[s15] V  Photons and quantized energy
  img: fig:levels / a:rgb_led
  • Light comes in packets: E = hf
  • Photoelectric effect: only high-enough f frees electrons
  • Atoms have discrete energy levels → sharp spectral lines
  • LED color = semiconductor band gap
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Light is also a particle.
     1: Each photon carries energy h times f.
     2: Shine dim blue light on certain metals, and electrons pop out.
     3: Shine intense red light, and nothing happens, because no single red photon has enough energy.
     4: Einstein explained this photoelectric effect in nineteen oh five, and it won him the Nobel Prize.
     5: Camera sensors and solar cells work on this principle: each photon can free one electron.
     6: And atoms can only hold electrons at specific, discrete energy levels.
     7: In hydrogen, they're minus thirteen point six electron volts over n squared.
     8: When an electron drops from one level to a lower one, it emits a photon with exactly that energy difference, which is why each element glows with its own fingerprint of colors.
  beat1:
     0: In a solid, those levels blur into bands separated by a gap, and the size of that gap decides whether a material is a conductor, an insulator or a semiconductor.
     1: An LED emits photons with energy roughly equal to its band gap.
     2: A handy rule: energy in electron volts is about twelve hundred forty divided by the wavelength in nanometers.
     3: So a red LED's band gap is about one point nine electron volts, and a blue LED's about two point eight.
     4: That's the bridge to the electronics course, where band gaps make diodes and transistors work.

[s17] B  Matter waves and the quantum world
  • de Broglie: particles have wavelength λ = h / p
  • Electron microscopes resolve atoms
  • Tunneling: electrons can pass through thin barriers
  • Flash memory stores bits by tunneling charge onto a floating gate
  beat0:
     0: One more twist.
     1: If waves can act like particles, particles can act like waves.
     2: Louis de Broglie proposed that every particle has a wavelength, h over its momentum.
  beat1:
     0: Electrons have wavelengths far shorter than visible light, which is why electron microscopes can image individual atoms, and why chipmakers use them to inspect their transistors.
  beat2:
     0: Waves can also leak through thin barriers that a classical particle could never cross.
     1: That's quantum tunneling.
  beat3:
     0: And you're using tunneling right now.
     1: Flash memory, in your phone and in every S S D, stores each bit by forcing electrons to tunnel through a very thin insulating layer onto an isolated floating gate, where they stay trapped for years.
     2: Quantum mechanics isn't just theory.
     3: It's in your pocket.
