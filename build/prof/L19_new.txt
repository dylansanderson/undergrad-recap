[s1] V  Signals: continuous and discrete
  img: fig:signals
  • Continuous x(t) vs discrete x[n] = x(nT)
  • Building blocks: step, impulse, exponential, sinusoid
  • Impulse δ(t): infinitely brief, area 1
  • Sifting: ∫ x(t) δ(t − t₀) dt = x(t₀)
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: A signal is any quantity that varies with time: a voltage, a sound pressure, a temperature.
     1: Continuous time signals are defined at every instant.
     2: Discrete time signals are sequences of samples, x of n, like the numbers an A D C produces.
  beat1:
     0: A few building blocks describe almost everything.
     1: The unit step turns on at time zero.
     2: The decaying exponential describes capacitor discharges.
     3: Sinusoids describe anything periodic.
  beat2:
     0: The strangest and most useful is the impulse, delta of t: an infinitely brief spike with an area of exactly one.
     1: Think of it as an idealized hammer tap.
  beat3:
     0: Its key property, called sifting: integrating any signal times a shifted impulse picks out the signal's value at that instant.
     1: That's why, if you know how a system responds to one impulse, you can predict its response to anything.

[s2] V  Systems and the magic of LTI
  img: fig:lti
  • Linear: scale & add inputs → outputs scale & add
  • Time-invariant: behaves the same at any time
  • Causal (no peeking at the future) · BIBO stable
  • LTI → completely described by its impulse response h(t)
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: A system takes an input signal and produces an output signal: an amplifier, a filter, a room's echo, a car's suspension.
  beat1:
     0: The most important class is linear and time invariant, L T I.
     1: Linear means superposition holds: double the input, double the output, and the response to a sum is the sum of the responses.
     2: Time invariant means the system behaves the same today as tomorrow.
  beat2:
     0: We also care whether a system is causal, meaning the output doesn't depend on future inputs, which every real time system must be, and whether it's stable: bounded inputs always give bounded outputs.
  beat3:
     0: Here's why L T I systems are so special.
     1: Any input can be written as a sum of shifted, scaled impulses.
     2: So if you know the response to one impulse, h of t, you know the response to everything.
     3: The output is the input convolved with h.
     4: And in the frequency domain, convolution becomes simple multiplication: Y equals H times X.
     5: That single fact is the reason transforms exist.

[s3] V  Convolution: flip, shift, multiply, add
  img: fig:conv0 / fig:conv1 / fig:conv2 / fig:conv3
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Convolution sounds intimidating, so let's watch it.
     1: The input x is a one second rectangular pulse, in blue.
     2: The system's impulse response is a decaying exponential, like an R C circuit.
     3: To find the output at time t, flip h around and slide it to position t, the orange curve.
     4: Multiply the two, and add up the overlapping area.
     5: At t equals point three, there's only a small overlap.
  beat1:
     0: Slide further.
     1: At point eight, the overlap is bigger, so the output keeps rising.
  beat2:
     0: At one point five, the pulse has ended, and the overlap is shrinking.
     1: The output is now decaying.
  beat3:
     0: By two and a half, it's almost gone.
     1: Plot the area at every position, and you get the purple output: the R C circuit charging during the pulse and discharging afterward, exactly what you'd measure on a scope.
     2: In discrete time it's the same idea with sums instead of integrals: y of n equals the sum over k of x of k times h of n minus k.
     3: Every digital filter computes exactly that.

[s5] V  Fourier series: any periodic signal is a sum of sines
  img: a:fourier / fig:fs1 / fig:fs3 / fig:fs9 / fig:fs25
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: In the early eighteen hundreds, Joseph Fourier claimed that any periodic signal can be written as a sum of sines and cosines at whole number multiples of a fundamental frequency, called harmonics.
     1: Mathematicians doubted him at first.
     2: He was right.
  beat1:
     0: Let's build a square wave.
     1: Start with one sine wave at the fundamental frequency.
     2: It's a rough approximation.
  beat2:
     0: Add the third harmonic, at one third the amplitude, and the fifth at one fifth.
     1: The edges sharpen.
  beat3:
     0: Nine terms, and it's clearly square.
  beat4:
     0: Twenty five terms, and it hugs the square wave closely.
     1: A square wave contains only odd harmonics, with amplitudes falling off as one over k.
     2: But notice the little spikes at each edge, about nine percent of the jump.
     3: They get narrower with more terms, but never shorter.
     4: That's the Gibbs phenomenon.
     5: This is why a digital clock signal, which is roughly a square wave, has energy at many harmonics, and why fast digital signals can cause radio interference at multiples of their clock frequency.

[s6] V  The Fourier transform: seeing frequencies
  img: fig:spectrum
  • X(ω) = ∫ x(t) e^(−jωt) dt
  • Time domain ⇄ frequency domain: same signal, two views
  • Convolution in time = multiplication in frequency
  • Frequency response H(jω): gain & phase per frequency
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: For signals that aren't periodic, the Fourier transform generalizes the series.
     1: It measures how much of each frequency is present in a signal.
  beat1:
     0: Look at this signal.
     1: In the time domain, on top, it's a messy wiggle.
     2: In the frequency domain, on the bottom, it's obvious: three tones, at four hundred forty, eight hundred eighty and thirteen twenty hertz, the note A and two of its harmonics.
     3: Same information, two views.
     4: Engineers switch between them constantly.
  beat2:
     0: The most important property: convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency.
     1: So to understand a filter, look at its frequency response, H, and multiply.
  beat3:
     0: The frequency response tells you, for each frequency, how much the system amplifies or attenuates it, and how much it shifts its phase.
     1: An audio equalizer, like the sliders on a mixing console, is just a system whose frequency response you can shape by hand.

[s7] V  Modulation: how radio shares the air
  img: fig:am
  • Multiply a message by a carrier cos(2πf_c t)
  • Its spectrum shifts up to ±f_c
  • Different stations → different f_c → no overlap
  • AM radio: 540–1700 kHz; Wi-Fi: 2.4 / 5 GHz
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's a beautiful application of the Fourier transform.
     1: Multiply a message signal by a high frequency cosine, the carrier.
     2: On top, you can see the message, in orange, as the envelope of the fast carrier.
     3: That's amplitude modulation, or A M.
  beat1:
     0: In the frequency domain, multiplying by a cosine simply shifts the message's spectrum up to plus and minus the carrier frequency.
  beat2:
     0: So many stations can share the air at the same time, each shifted to its own carrier frequency, as long as their shifted spectra don't overlap.
     1: A radio receiver uses a band pass filter to pick out one station, then shifts it back down.
  beat3:
     0: A M radio stations sit between about five hundred forty and seventeen hundred kilohertz.
     1: Wi Fi uses carriers around two point four and five gigahertz, with far more sophisticated modulation, but the same principle.
     2: In this course's second assignment, students generate a carrier, a message and the modulated signal in MATLAB and look at their spectra.

[s8] V  Sampling and aliasing
  img: fig:alias
  • Sampling at f_s repeats the spectrum every f_s
  • Nyquist: need f_s > 2 f_max
  • Too slow → high frequencies masquerade as low ones
  • Anti-alias low-pass filter before every ADC
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: When an A D C samples a signal f s times per second, the sampled signal's spectrum repeats every f s.
     1: If the signal contains frequencies above half the sampling rate, those copies overlap.
  beat1:
     0: So the Nyquist sampling theorem says: to capture a signal whose highest frequency is f max, you must sample faster than two times f max.
     1: That's why audio CDs sample at forty four point one kilohertz, a bit more than twice the twenty kilohertz upper limit of human hearing.
  beat2:
     0: Break the rule and something sneaky happens.
     1: Here, a nine hertz signal is sampled at only ten hertz.
     2: The red samples fit a one hertz sine wave perfectly, so that's what you'd reconstruct.
     3: The high frequency has disguised itself as a low one.
     4: That's aliasing, and it's the same reason wagon wheels in movies appear to spin backwards.
  beat3:
     0: Once aliasing happens, it can't be undone, because the samples are identical.
     1: So every A D C needs an analog low pass filter in front of it, an anti aliasing filter, to remove frequencies above half the sampling rate before sampling.

[s10] V  The Laplace transform: poles decide stability
  img: fig:splane
  • X(s) = ∫₀^∞ x(t) e^(−st) dt, s = σ + jω
  • Derivatives become multiplication by s → ODEs become algebra
  • H(s) = N(s)/D(s); poles = roots of D(s)
  • All poles in the left half-plane → stable
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: The Laplace transform generalizes Fourier by using a complex frequency, s, equals sigma plus j omega.
     1: Its superpower: differentiation becomes multiplication by s, so the differential equations from math two fifty B turn into algebra.
     2: Solve for the output, then transform back with partial fractions, the technique from Calculus two.
  beat1:
     0: Every L T I system gets a transfer function, H of s, a ratio of polynomials.
     1: The roots of the denominator are the poles, and they're exactly the eigenvalues from linear algebra.
     2: Each pole contributes a term to the impulse response.
     3: A pole at minus two point two gives a smooth exponential decay.
     4: A pair at minus one plus or minus two j gives a decaying oscillation: the real part sets the decay rate, the imaginary part sets the ringing frequency.
     5: A pole at plus one gives exponential growth: an unstable system.
     6: So the rule is simple: a system is stable if and only if all its poles are in the left half of the s plane.
     7: Every amplifier, power supply, drone flight controller and cruise control is designed around this picture.

[s12] V  Frequency response and Bode plots
  img: fig:bode_rc
  • Evaluate H(s) at s = jω
  • RC low-pass: H = 1 / (1 + jωRC)
  • 1 kΩ, 100 nF → f_c ≈ 1.6 kHz, then −20 dB/decade
  • Bode: magnitude in dB and phase vs log frequency
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: To get a system's frequency response, evaluate its transfer function on the imaginary axis, at s equals j omega.
     1: Take the simple R C low pass filter: H equals one over one plus j omega R C.
     2: With one kilohm and one hundred nanofarads, the corner frequency is one over two pi R C, about one point six kilohertz.
     3: Below that, signals pass almost unchanged.
     4: At the corner, the gain is minus three decibels, half power, and the phase is minus forty five degrees.
     5: Above it, the gain falls by twenty decibels for every factor of ten in frequency, and the phase approaches minus ninety degrees.
     6: A Bode plot shows exactly this: gain in decibels and phase, against frequency on a log scale.
     7: Engineers sketch Bode plots by hand from the poles and zeros, a skill you'll use for filters, amplifiers and feedback control.
     8: And watch the units, because this trips up everyone.
     9: Omega is in radians per second, and f is in hertz, cycles per second.
     10: Omega equals two pi f.
     11: The corner is at omega equals one over R C, which is f equals one over two pi R C.
     12: Forgetting the two pi is the most common mistake in this course.

[s14] V  Discrete time: the Z-transform
  img: fig:zplane
  • X(z) = Σ x[n] z⁻ⁿ; z⁻¹ = one-sample delay
  • y[n] = x[n] + a·y[n−1] → H(z) = 1 / (1 − a z⁻¹)
  • Stable iff all poles inside the unit circle
  • Connect: z = e^(sT)
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: For discrete time signals, the Z transform plays the role of Laplace.
     1: Its key idea: multiplying by z to the minus one means delay by one sample.
  beat1:
     0: So a difference equation, like y of n equals x of n plus a times y of n minus one, becomes H of z equals one over one minus a z to the minus one.
     1: That has a pole at z equals a.
  beat2:
     0: In discrete time, the stability boundary is the unit circle.
     1: A pole inside it, like point eight, gives an impulse response of point eight to the n, decaying.
     2: A pole outside it, like one point two, grows forever.
  beat3:
     0: The two worlds are connected by z equals e to the s T, which maps the left half of the s plane to the inside of the unit circle.
     1: In the course assignments, students compute Z transforms and plot poles and zeros in MATLAB, exactly like this.

[s16] V  Digital filters, the DFT and the FFT
  img: fig:filters
  • FIR: weighted sum of recent inputs — always stable
  • IIR: feeds back old outputs — sharp, cheap, can go unstable
  • DFT: spectrum of N samples; FFT computes it in O(N log N)
  • Frequency resolution = f_s / N
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Digital filters are just difference equations running on a processor.
     1: A finite impulse response, or F I R, filter is a weighted sum of recent inputs.
     2: The simplest is a moving average: here, averaging nine samples smooths a noisy sensor reading.
     3: F I R filters are always stable and can have perfectly linear phase.
  beat1:
     0: An infinite impulse response, or I I R, filter also feeds back old outputs.
     1: This one blends eighty five percent of the previous output with fifteen percent of the new input.
     2: With just one multiply per sample, it smooths as well as a long F I R filter.
     3: The trade off: I I R filters have poles, so a bad design can be unstable.
  beat2:
     0: To analyze sampled data, we use the discrete Fourier transform, the D F T, which computes the spectrum of N samples.
     1: Done directly, it takes N squared operations.
     2: The fast Fourier transform, the F F T, computes the same result in N log N, the divide and conquer trick from data structures.
     3: It's been called one of the most important algorithms of the twentieth century.
  beat3:
     0: The frequency resolution is the sample rate divided by N: one second of audio at forty four point one kilohertz resolves frequencies one hertz apart.
     1: F F Ts run in every phone, Wi Fi chip, M P 3 encoder and spectrum analyzer.
