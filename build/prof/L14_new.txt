[s2] V  Where did VHDL come from?
  img: fig:timeline
  beat0:
     0: V H D L stands for V H S I C Hardware Description Language, and V H S I C stands for Very High Speed Integrated Circuit.
     1: It was a U S Department of Defense program in the early nineteen eighties.
     2: The military was buying chips from many suppliers, and the documentation was written in plain English, which was ambiguous.
     3: Worse, when a part became obsolete, nobody could rebuild it exactly.
  beat1:
     0: So the D o D funded a language that could describe a chip so precisely that a computer could simulate it.

     3: Around the same time, a company called Gateway Design Automation created Verilog, which has a shorter, C like syntax.
     4: Both languages are still alive, both are I E E E standards, and most professional engineers can read both.

[s4] V  What is an FPGA?
  img: fig:fpga / fig:lut / a:fpga_chip
  • Field-Programmable Gate Array
  • Thousands to millions of small **logic blocks**
  • Each block: a **look-up table** + a flip-flop
  • Programmable wiring connects them
  beat0:
     0: Since V H D L is so often used with F P G As, let's open one up.
     1: F P G A stands for field programmable gate array.
     2: Field programmable means you can reconfigure it after it leaves the factory, right on your desk.
  beat1:
     0: Inside is a grid of small logic blocks, shown in blue.
     1: Between them is a mesh of programmable wiring, the orange lines, with switches where wires cross.
     2: Around the edge are the input and output pins, in green.
  beat2:
     0: Here's the clever part.
     1: Each logic block contains a look-up table, which is just a tiny memory that holds a truth table.
     2: This one has three inputs, so it has eight memory cells, one for every input combination.
     3: Store the right eight bits and it becomes any three input logic function you want.
     4: Here it's programmed as a majority function.
     5: Next to each look-up table is a flip-flop, so the block can also remember a bit.
  beat3:
     0: So when you program an F P G A, you're loading a configuration file that fills in all of those truth tables and closes all the right wiring switches.
     1: In about a second, this chip becomes your circuit.
     2: Change your code, reload, and it becomes a different circuit.
     3: That's why F P G As are perfect for learning, prototyping, and products that need updates.

[s7] EX  Anatomy of a design: entity + architecture
  img: fig:entity
  CODE:
    library ieee;
    use ieee.std_logic_1164.all;
    
    entity full_adder is
      port ( a, b, cin : in  std_logic;
             s, cout   : out std_logic );
    end full_adder;
    
    architecture dataflow of full_adder is
    begin
      s    <= a xor b xor cin;
      cout <= (a and b) or (cin and (a xor b));
    end dataflow;
  (pen marks: 9 )
  beat0:
     0: Every V H D L design has the same skeleton.
     1: First, we include the I E E E library, which gives us the standard logic type we'll use for wires.
  beat1:
     0: Next is the entity.
     1: Think of it as the outside of the chip: its name, and its ports, which are the pins.
     2: Each port has a direction, in or out, and a type.
     3: Our full adder has three inputs, a, b and carry in, and two outputs, sum and carry out.
     4: Look at the diagram on the right: the entity is exactly that black box with five pins.
  beat2:
     0: Then the architecture describes what's inside the box.
     1: Here we use two concurrent assignment statements, one for the sum and one for the carry.
     2: The arrow made of a less than sign and an equals sign means: this signal is driven by this logic.
     3: Remember, both lines are hardware that exists at the same time.
     4: The order you write them in doesn't matter at all.

[s8] V  Designing a full adder, step by step
  img: fig:fa_table / fig:fa_table / fig:fa_gates / fig:fa_table_hl
  • Add three bits: a + b + carry-in
  • **Sum** is 1 when an odd number of inputs are 1 → XOR
  • **Carry** is 1 when two or more inputs are 1
  • Check: 1 + 1 + 0 = binary 10 = 2 ✓
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: But where did those equations come from?
     1: Let's derive them, the way you would on an exam.
     2: A full adder adds three single bits: a, b, and a carry coming in from the previous column.
     3: The result is two bits, a carry out and a sum.
  beat1:
     0: Here's the full truth table, all eight combinations.
     1: Look at the sum column.
     2: It's one whenever an odd number of the inputs are one.
     3: That's exactly what X O R does, so the sum is a X O R b X O R carry in.
  beat2:
     0: Now the carry column.
     1: It's one when at least two inputs are one.
     2: You can write that as: a AND b, OR carry in AND, a X O R b.
     3: Here's that circuit drawn as gates.
     4: Two X O R gates make the sum.
     5: Two AND gates and an OR gate make the carry.
     6: Notice the first X O R is shared by both outputs.
     7: Real designers love sharing logic, because it saves gates.
  beat3:
     0: Let's check one row.
     1: a equals one, b equals one, carry in equals zero.
     2: The sum: one X O R one is zero, X O R zero is still zero.
     3: The carry: a AND b is one, so carry out is one.
     4: Reading carry then sum gives one zero, which is binary for two.
     5: And one plus one plus zero is two.
     6: The table, the equations, and the circuit all agree.

[s9] V  Real gates have delay — and delay causes glitches
  img: fig:glitch
  • Every gate takes time: **propagation delay**
  • Inputs that change at slightly different times → brief wrong output
  • VHDL can model it:  s <= a xor b after 2 ns;
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's something software never makes you think about.
     1: Every real gate takes time to respond, usually a few picoseconds to nanoseconds.
     2: That's called propagation delay.
  beat1:
     0: Now look at this timing diagram.
     1: Input a rises, and b is supposed to fall at the same moment, but b's path is a little slower.
     2: For a brief instant, both a and b are one, so a X O R b drops to zero, then pops back up.
     3: That spike is called a glitch, or a hazard.
     4: The final answer is right, but for a moment the output lied.
  beat2:
     0: V H D L lets you model this with the keyword after, like s gets a X O R b after two nanoseconds.
     1: In this course's first lab, students add delays like that to a full adder and watch glitches appear in the simulator.
     2: Later we'll see the professional fix: synchronous design, where we only look at outputs on a clock edge, after everything has settled.

[s10] V  Structural design: chaining four full adders
  img: fig:rca0 / fig:rca1 / fig:rca2 / fig:rca3 / fig:rca4
  beat0:
     0: One full adder adds one column.
     1: To add whole numbers, we copy it.
     2: Here are four full adders in a row, making a four bit ripple carry adder.
     3: Each carry out feeds the next adder's carry in, exactly like carrying a one in grade school addition.
     4: Let's add seven plus three: zero one one one, plus zero zero one one.
  beat1:
     0: Column zero: one plus one plus a carry in of zero gives sum zero, carry one.
  beat2:
     0: Column one: one plus one plus that carry of one gives sum one, carry one.
  beat3:
     0: Column two: one plus zero plus one gives sum zero, carry one.
  beat4:
     0: Column three: zero plus zero plus one gives sum one, carry zero.
     1: Reading the sums from left to right: one zero one zero, which is ten.
     2: Seven plus three is ten.
     3: Notice the catch, though.
     4: Each stage had to wait for the carry from its neighbor.
     5: The carry ripples through, so a thirty two bit ripple adder waits through thirty two stages.
     6: That's why real processors use faster carry look ahead adders, which compute all the carries in parallel.

[s11] EX  Port maps: wiring components like a schematic
  • Internal **signals** are the wires between components
  • **port map** connects each pin to a wire
  • **for … generate** stamps out copies — 4 or 64 adders, same code
  CODE:
    architecture structural of adder4 is
      signal c : std_logic_vector(4 downto 0);
    begin
      c(0) <= cin;
      stages: for i in 0 to 3 generate
        fa: entity work.full_adder
          port map ( a => a(i), b => b(i), cin => c(i),
                     s => s(i), cout => c(i+1) );
      end generate;
      cout <= c(4);
    end structural;
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's how you build that in V H D L.
     1: This is called structural design.
     2: First, declare internal signals, which are wires.
     3: Here, c is a five bit bus of carry wires.
     4: Then instantiate the full adder four times.
     5: The port map connects each pin of the component to a wire: a goes to a of i, carry in goes to c of i, carry out goes to c of i plus one.
     6: And instead of typing it four times, a for generate loop stamps out the copies.
     7: Change the three to a sixty three and you have a sixty four bit adder.
     8: This is exactly how big designs are built: small, tested pieces, wired together like a schematic.
     9: In this course's second lab, students build this adder and then extend it.

[s12] V  Negative numbers: two's complement
  img: fig:wheel / fig:addsub
  • −x = (NOT x) + 1
  • 4 bits hold −8 … +7
  • Subtract = add the complement → one adder does both
  • Overflow: carry into MSB ≠ carry out
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: How does hardware handle negative numbers?
     1: Almost every computer uses two's complement.
     2: Picture the sixteen four bit patterns arranged on a wheel.
     3: Adding one means stepping clockwise.
     4: The top half of the patterns represents zero through seven, and the patterns with a leading one represent minus eight through minus one.
     5: To negate a number, flip every bit and add one.
     6: Five is zero one zero one.
     7: Flip it to get one zero one zero, add one, and you get one zero one one, which is minus five.
     8: The beautiful part is that the same adder circuit works for negative numbers with no changes.
     9: But watch the orange line.
     10: Seven plus one wraps around to minus eight.
     11: That's overflow: the true answer didn't fit in four bits.
     12: The hardware detects it when the carry into the top bit differs from the carry out of it.
  beat1:
     0: And here's the payoff.
     1: Since A minus B equals A plus NOT B plus one, we can build a subtractor from our adder with almost no extra hardware.
     2: Put an X O R gate on each B input, controlled by a single SUB line.
     3: When SUB is zero, X O R passes B through unchanged and the circuit adds.
     4: When SUB is one, X O R flips every bit of B, and SUB also feeds the carry in, supplying the plus one.
     5: One control wire turns an adder into a subtractor.
     6: That's the heart of every A L U you'll ever meet.

[s14] WB  Numbers in VHDL: bits vs unsigned vs signed
  step0 board+: signal count : std_logic_vector(3 downto 0); || count <= count + 1;     -- ERROR: no "+" for plain bits || PEN ('cross',) on count <= count + 1;     -- ERROR
     0: Here's the error every beginner hits in their first hour.
     1: You write count gets count plus one, and the compiler refuses.
     2: Why?
     3: Because a standard logic vector is just a bundle of wires.
     4: It has no idea whether those bits are a number, let alone a positive or negative one.
  step1 board+: use ieee.numeric_std.all; || 1011  as unsigned = 11      as signed = −5 || PEN ('underline',) on 1011  as unsigned
     0: The fix is the numeric standard library.
     1: It adds two types made of the same bits.
     2: Unsigned means the bits are a plain binary number.
     3: Signed means they're two's complement.
     4: The same pattern, one zero one one, is eleven as unsigned, and minus five as signed.
  step2 board+: signal count : unsigned(3 downto 0); || count <= count + 1;                        -- OK || PEN ('check',) on count <= count + 1;                        || n   := to_integer(count); || cnt := to_unsigned(9, 4);                  -- 9 as 4 bits || leds <= std_logic_vector(count);
     0: So declare counters as unsigned, and plus just works.
     1: When you need to move between worlds, convert explicitly.
     2: To integer turns unsigned into an integer.
     3: To unsigned, with a width, goes the other way.
     4: And wrapping a value in standard logic vector turns it back into plain bits for a port.
  step3 board+: a <= '1';        -- one bit: single quotes || v <= "0101";     -- a vector: double quotes
     0: And one small rule that causes a lot of syntax errors: a single bit uses single quotes, like quote one quote.
     1: A vector uses double quotes, like quote zero one zero one quote.
     2: Mix them up, and the compiler complains about types.

[s15] V  std_logic: a wire has nine possible values
  img: fig:stdlogic
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: In V H D L, wires usually have the type standard logic, and it has nine possible values, not two.
     1: That sounds strange, but it's because real wires do more than zero and one.
     2: The important ones are highlighted.
     3: Capital U means uninitialized.
     4: If you see U in your simulation, something never got a value, usually because you forgot a reset.
     5: Capital X means unknown, and it usually means two different circuits are driving the same wire, one pushing a zero and the other a one.
     6: That's a real short circuit in hardware, so treat X as an alarm bell.
     7: Capital Z means high impedance: the driver has disconnected itself.
     8: That's how many devices can share one bus, taking turns, with everyone else in Z.
     9: The weak values model pull up and pull down resistors, and the dash means don't care, which gives the synthesis tool freedom to optimize.
     10: Rule of thumb: a clean simulation shows only zeros and ones where it matters.

[s16] EX  Three ways to write combinational logic
  img: fig:mux
  CODE:
    -- 1. simple concurrent assignment
    y1 <= (a and b) or c;
    
    -- 2. conditional: when / else  (priority)
    y2 <= a when sel = "00" else
          b when sel = "01" else
          c when sel = "10" else d;
    
    -- 3. selected: with / select  (a true mux)
    with sel select
      y3 <= a when "00",
            b when "01",
            c when "10",
            d when others;
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: For combinational logic, logic with no memory, V H D L gives you three styles outside of processes.
     1: The first is the plain concurrent assignment you've already seen: a Boolean equation.
  beat1:
     0: The second is the conditional assignment, using when and else.
     1: It checks conditions in order, so it naturally builds a priority structure: the first true condition wins.
  beat2:
     0: The third is the selected assignment, with select.
     1: Every choice has equal priority, so it maps directly onto a multiplexer, like the four to one mux on the right.
     2: The two select bits choose which of the four inputs reaches the output.
     3: Notice the when others line at the end.
     4: Standard logic has nine values, so two select bits have far more than four combinations.
     5: When others covers all the rest, and it keeps the synthesis tool from inventing memory.
     6: You'll see why that matters in a minute.

[s18] EX  Processes: how VHDL describes memory
  img: fig:dff
  CODE:
    process (clk)
    begin
      if rising_edge(clk) then
        if reset = '1' then
          q <= '0';
        else
          q <= d;
        end if;
      end if;
    end process;
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: So far, everything has been combinational.
     1: Now let's add memory.
     2: In V H D L, memory is described inside a process.
     3: A process is a block whose statements read top to bottom, like software, but it still becomes hardware.
     4: The list in parentheses is the sensitivity list: the process wakes up whenever one of those signals changes.
  beat1:
     0: The key phrase is if rising edge of clock.
     1: Any signal you assign inside that if becomes a flip-flop, a one bit memory that only updates on the clock's rising edge.
     2: Look at the timing diagram.
     3: The input d can wiggle whenever it likes, but q only copies d at the instant the clock goes from zero to one, then holds that value until the next edge.
     4: That's the foundation of every register, counter and state machine.
     5: The reset branch gives the flip-flop a known starting value, which is how you avoid those U values in simulation.
     6: In this course's fourth lab, students build flip-flops, then registers, then a small accumulator machine out of them.

[s19] EX  Signals vs. variables
  img: fig:swap
  CODE:
    -- signals: update when the process suspends
    process (clk) begin
      if rising_edge(clk) then
        a <= b;      -- uses OLD b
        b <= a;      -- uses OLD a  → swap!
      end if;
    end process;
    
    -- variables: update immediately
    process (x)
      variable t : integer;
    begin
      t := x * 2;    -- t changes right now
      y <= t + 1;
    end process;
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's a subtle rule that trips up every beginner.
     1: Inside a process, a signal assignment doesn't take effect immediately.
     2: All signal updates are scheduled, and they happen together when the process finishes.
     3: So look at the top example: a gets b, and b gets a.
     4: In software, that would make both equal.
     5: In V H D L, both assignments use the old values, so the two registers genuinely swap on every clock edge, just like the picture: two flip-flops feeding each other.
     6: That's exactly how real hardware behaves, because both flip-flops sample at the same instant.
     7: Variables are different.
     8: They use the colon equals symbol, they live only inside the process, and they update immediately, like software.
     9: Use signals for wires and registers.
     10: Use variables only for temporary calculations inside a process.

[s20] EX  The #1 beginner bug: the accidental latch
  img: fig:latch
  CODE:
    -- BAD: no else branch
    process (a, en) begin
      if en = '1' then
        y <= a;
      end if;        -- en = '0' → y must
    end process;     -- REMEMBER → latch!
    
    -- GOOD: default first
    process (a, en) begin
      y <= '0';      -- default value
      if en = '1' then
        y <= a;
      end if;
    end process;
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Now the most common bug in all of beginner H D L.
     1: Look at the top process.
     2: If enable is one, y gets a.
     3: But what happens to y when enable is zero?
     4: The code doesn't say.
     5: In software, nothing happens and y keeps its old value.
     6: In hardware, keeping an old value requires memory, so the synthesis tool quietly builds a latch, a level sensitive memory element, to remember it.
     7: You meant to build a simple gate and you got a storage element with tricky timing.
     8: The tool usually just prints a warning that's easy to miss.
  beat1:
     0: The fix is simple.
     1: In a combinational process, give every output a default value at the very top, then override it in the branches.
     2: Now y is defined on every path, so the tool builds pure logic: a multiplexer that outputs a or zero.
     3: The two rules for combinational processes: list every input in the sensitivity list, or use the V H D L twenty oh eight keyword all, and assign every output on every path.

[s21] V  How fast can your design run?
  img: fig:timingpath
  • Data leaves a flip-flop (clock-to-Q)
  • Travels through logic
  • Must arrive **before** the next edge (setup)
  • f max = 1 / (t clk→q + t logic + t setup)
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Every synchronous design is a loop: flip-flop, then some combinational logic, then another flip-flop.
     1: On each clock edge, data leaves the first flip-flop.
     2: That takes the clock to Q delay, say half a nanosecond.
  beat1:
     0: Then it travels through the logic.
     1: Say the slowest path, called the critical path, takes three nanoseconds.
  beat2:
     0: It has to arrive at the next flip-flop a little before the next clock edge.
     1: That's the setup time, say zero point three nanoseconds.
  beat3:
     0: Add them up: three point eight nanoseconds is the shortest legal clock period.
     1: One divided by three point eight nanoseconds is about two hundred sixty three megahertz.
     2: That's the maximum clock frequency.
     3: If you want it faster, you have to shorten the critical path, for example by using a faster adder, or by splitting the logic in half with another register in the middle.
     4: That trick is called pipelining, and it's how processors reach gigahertz speeds.
     5: The tools report this for you after place and route, in a timing report, and learning to read it is a real job skill.

[s23] EX  Clock dividers: slowing 100 MHz down to human speed
  img: fig:clkdiv
  CODE:
    process (clk) begin
      if rising_edge(clk) then
        if count = N - 1 then
          count   <= 0;
          slow_clk <= not slow_clk;
        else
          count <= count + 1;
        end if;
      end if;
    end process;
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Lab boards usually run at a hundred megahertz, a hundred million ticks per second.
     1: If you blink an L E D at that rate, it just looks dimly on.
     2: So we build a clock divider: a counter that counts input cycles and flips a slow output every N cycles.
     3: Each full cycle of the output takes two N input cycles, so the output frequency is the input frequency divided by two N.
     4: In the diagram, N is four, so the output is eight times slower.
     5: Now a real calculation.
     6: To get one hertz from a hundred megahertz, N must be fifty million.
     7: How many bits does the counter need?
     8: Two to the twenty fifth is about thirty three million, too small.
     9: Two to the twenty sixth is about sixty seven million, enough.
     10: So you need a twenty six bit counter.
     11: One professional tip: in real designs, we usually don't use the divided signal as a new clock.
     12: We generate a one cycle enable pulse instead and keep the whole design on a single clock.
     13: It avoids a whole class of timing problems.

[s24] V  Multiplexed displays: tricking your eyes
  img: a:sevenseg / fig:muxdisp
  • 4 digits share the same 7 segment wires
  • Light **one digit at a time**
  • Cycle at ~1 kHz → persistence of vision
  (pen marks: 1 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's a lovely engineering trick that combines everything so far.
     1: A four digit display has four times seven, which is twenty eight L E Ds.
     2: Giving each one its own pin would waste pins.
  beat1:
     0: So all four digits share the same seven segment wires, and each digit gets its own enable line, called an anode.
     1: A circuit turns on one digit at a time, putting that digit's pattern on the shared segment lines, then moves to the next digit, about a thousand times per second.
     2: Each digit is actually lit only a quarter of the time, but your eye blends it together, an effect called persistence of vision, so you see all four steadily.
     3: In this course's sixth lab, students build exactly this: a clock divider, a counter that selects the digit, a multiplexer for the data, and the seven segment decoder from earlier.
     4: It's a small but complete digital system.

[s25] V  Finite state machines
  img: fig:fsm101 / fig:fsm101 / fig:fsm101_s3 / fig:mealy
  • State register + next-state logic + output logic
  • Example: detect the bit pattern 1-0-1
  • **Moore:** outputs depend only on the state
  • **Mealy:** outputs depend on state **and** input
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Now the most useful design pattern in all of digital logic: the finite state machine.
     1: Every state machine has three parts: a register holding the current state, logic that computes the next state from the current state and the inputs, and logic that computes the outputs.
  beat1:
     0: Let's design one.
     1: A serial input delivers one bit per clock cycle, and we want to raise an output whenever the last three bits were one, zero, one.
     2: We start in IDLE.
     3: If we see a one, we move to got one.
     4: From there, a zero moves us to got one zero.
     5: A one in got one also keeps us there, because that one could be the start of a new pattern.
  beat2:
     0: From got one zero, a one completes the pattern, so we go to FOUND, where the output is one.
     1: A zero there breaks the pattern and sends us back to IDLE.
     2: And from FOUND, the final one can begin a new pattern, so a following zero takes us straight to got one zero.
     3: This is a Moore machine, because the output depends only on which state we're in.
  beat3:
     0: Here's the Mealy version.
     1: Outputs are written on the arrows as input slash output, because they depend on the current state and the input.
     2: The output fires on the transition itself, the orange arrow, so we don't need a FOUND state.
     3: That's one fewer state, and the answer arrives one cycle sooner.
     4: The trade off is that a Mealy output can glitch if the input glitches, while a Moore output is clean.
     5: Simplicity versus speed is a trade off you'll make over and over as an engineer.

[s27] EX  Writing a state machine in VHDL
  img: fig:traffic
  CODE:
    type state_t is (NS_GREEN, NS_YELLOW,
                     EW_GREEN, EW_YELLOW);
    signal state : state_t;
    
    process (clk) begin
      if rising_edge(clk) then
        if reset = '1' then state <= NS_GREEN;
        else
          case state is
            when NS_GREEN  => if t = 30 then
                                state <= NS_YELLOW; end if;
            when NS_YELLOW => if t = 4 then
                                state <= EW_GREEN; end if;
            -- ... and so on
          end case;
        end if;
      end if;
    end process;
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Let's turn a real controller into code: a traffic light at an intersection, a classic state machine.
     1: There are four states: north south green, north south yellow, east west green, and east west yellow.
     2: Each state lasts a set number of seconds, which we measure with a counter that counts ticks of a divided clock.
     3: In V H D L, you first declare an enumerated type listing the states by name, which makes the code readable and lets the tool choose the binary encoding.
     4: Then a clocked process uses a case statement: in each state, check the condition, and move to the next state when it's met.
     5: The outputs, which lights are on, come from the state.
     6: Real intersections add sensor inputs, for example staying green for the main road until a car arrives on the side street, which is just another condition on an arrow.
     7: In this course's fifth lab, students design state machines like this, including counters and a controller, in Vivado.

[s28] EX  Test benches: prove it before you build it
  img: fig:tb
  CODE:
    entity tb is end tb;      -- no ports!
    
    architecture sim of tb is
      signal a, b, cin, s, cout : std_logic;
    begin
      dut: entity work.full_adder
        port map (a, b, cin, s, cout);
    
      process
        variable v : unsigned(2 downto 0);
      begin
        for i in 0 to 7 loop
          v := to_unsigned(i, 3);
          a <= v(2); b <= v(1); cin <= v(0);
          wait for 10 ns;
          assert (s = (a xor b xor cin))
            report "sum wrong" severity error;
        end loop;
        wait;
      end process;
    end sim;
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Finally, how do professionals know their design works?
     1: A test bench.
     2: It's a V H D L entity with no ports, a closed world that exists only in the simulator and is never turned into hardware.
     3: Inside, it instantiates your design, called the D U T, the design under test.
     4: A process then drives the inputs.
     5: Here, a loop walks through all eight combinations, waits ten nanoseconds for things to settle, and uses assert to check the answer.
     6: If the output is wrong, the simulator prints an error message.
     7: That's a self checking test bench: instead of staring at waveforms and hoping you notice a mistake, the test tells you.
     8: In industry, verification engineers often outnumber design engineers, because finding a bug before manufacturing is so valuable.
     9: Wait statements like wait for ten nanoseconds only make sense in simulation, which is one reason a test bench can't be synthesized.

[s29] B  Pro tips and common mistakes
  • Draw the circuit (or block diagram) **before** coding
  • Combinational process: default every output — no latches
  • Clocked process: one clock, reset everything
  • Treat **U** and **X** in simulation as bugs
  • Read the synthesis warnings and the timing report
  • Write a self-checking test bench for every module
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Let's collect the habits that separate working designs from frustrating ones.
  beat1:
     0: First, draw the circuit or at least a block diagram before you write code.
     1: Code is the schematic written in words.
  beat2:
     0: Second, in combinational processes, give every output a default, so you never infer latches.
  beat3:
     0: Third, in clocked processes, use one clock for the whole design and reset everything that matters.
  beat4:
     0: Fourth, if your simulation shows U or X, stop and find out why.
     1: It's almost always a missing reset or two drivers on one wire.
  beat5:
     0: Fifth, read the warnings and the timing report.
     1: The tools tell you about latches, unused signals and slow paths, but only if you look.
  beat6:
     0: And sixth, write a test bench for every module, ideally self checking.
     1: A small tested module is worth more than a big untested one.
