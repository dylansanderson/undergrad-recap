[s3] EX  Integration by parts: the product rule in reverse
  img: fig:xex
  CODE:
    ∫ u dv = u·v − ∫ v du
    
    Example:  ∫ x·eˣ dx
      u  = x      dv = eˣ dx
      du = dx     v  = eˣ
    
    = x·eˣ − ∫ eˣ dx
    = x·eˣ − eˣ + C
    
    Pick u by LIATE:
    Log, Inverse trig, Algebraic,
    Trig, Exponential
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: The first tool is integration by parts.
     1: It comes from the product rule, run backwards, and it says the integral of u d v equals u times v, minus the integral of v d u.
     2: The idea is to trade an integral you can't do for one you can.
     3: Example: the integral of x times e to the x.
     4: Choose u equals x, because its derivative is simpler, just one.
     5: Then d v is e to the x d x, so v is e to the x.
     6: Plug in: x e to the x, minus the integral of e to the x, which gives x e to the x minus e to the x, plus C.
     7: To choose u, a handy rule is LIATE: logs first, then inverse trig, then algebraic, then trig, then exponentials.
     8: Pick whichever comes first in that list as u.
     9: You'll use this exact technique in signals and systems to compute Laplace and Fourier transforms.

[s5] V  Trig integrals — and why your outlet says 120 volts
  img: fig:sin2
  • Half-angle identity: sin²t = ½ − ½cos 2t
  • Average of sin² over a cycle = ½
  • RMS voltage: V_rms = V_peak / √2
  • US outlet: 120 V rms → peaks at ≈ 170 V
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Integrals of powers of sine and cosine use trig identities.
     1: The most useful is the half angle identity: sine squared t equals one half minus one half cosine two t.
  beat1:
     0: Integrate that over a full cycle.
     1: The cosine part averages to zero, so the average of sine squared is exactly one half.
     2: You can see it in the graph: the blue sine squared wave bounces between zero and one, centered on one half.
  beat2:
     0: Here's why engineers care.
     1: The power delivered by an AC voltage depends on the voltage squared.
     2: So the effective voltage, called R M S for root mean square, is the square root of the average of the square.
     3: For a sine wave, that's the peak times the square root of one half, which is the peak divided by root two.
  beat3:
     0: That's why a U S wall outlet labeled one hundred twenty volts actually swings up to about one hundred seventy volts at its peak.
     1: One twenty times root two is about one seventy.
     2: That label comes straight from this integral.

[s7] EX  Partial fractions: break a fraction into simple pieces
  • Factor the bottom, split into simple fractions
  • Plug in the roots to find A and B instantly
  • Used constantly in circuits & signals (inverse Laplace transforms)
  CODE:
    ∫ 1 / ((x+1)(x+2)) dx
    
    1/((x+1)(x+2)) = A/(x+1) + B/(x+2)
    1 = A(x+2) + B(x+1)
    x = −1:  1 = A       x = −2:  1 = −B
    
    = ∫ [ 1/(x+1) − 1/(x+2) ] dx
    = ln|x+1| − ln|x+2| + C
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: For rational functions, one polynomial over another, we use partial fractions.
     1: Take one over x plus one times x plus two.
     2: Write it as A over x plus one, plus B over x plus two.
     3: Multiply through by the denominator: one equals A times x plus two, plus B times x plus one.
     4: Now a shortcut: plug in x equals negative one, and the B term vanishes, leaving one equals A.
     5: Plug in x equals negative two, and you get one equals negative B.
     6: So the integral splits into one over x plus one, minus one over x plus two, and each piece integrates to a natural log.
     7: Remember this one, because in signals and systems, the response of almost every circuit comes out as a fraction like this, and partial fractions is how you turn it back into a time signal.
     8: Two cautions for exams.
     9: The plug in shortcut works when the bottom splits into different linear factors and the top has a lower degree.
     10: If the top's degree is as big as the bottom's, do long division first.
     11: And a repeated factor, like x plus one squared, needs one term for each power: A over x plus one, plus B over x plus one squared.

[s8] V  Improper integrals: area out to infinity
  img: fig:improper
  • Replace ∞ with b, then take the limit
  • ∫ 1/x² from 1 to ∞ = 1 (finite!)
  • ∫ 1/x from 1 to ∞ = ∞ (diverges)
  • Key in probability and Laplace transforms
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Can an infinitely long region have a finite area?
     1: Surprisingly, yes.
     2: An improper integral has an infinite limit.
     3: To handle it, replace infinity with a variable b, integrate, then let b go to infinity.
  beat1:
     0: For one over x squared from one to infinity, the antiderivative is negative one over x.
     1: From one to b, that's one minus one over b.
     2: As b goes to infinity, that heads to one.
     3: Finite area!
  beat2:
     0: But one over x, which looks almost the same, has antiderivative ln x, which grows forever.
     1: Its area is infinite.
     2: The difference is how fast the curve drops.
  beat3:
     0: You'll use improper integrals all the time.
     1: In probability, the total area under a probability curve, over the whole number line, must equal one.
     2: And the Laplace transform, the main tool of signals and systems, is an integral from zero to infinity.

[s10] V  When there is no formula: numerical integration

  • Simpson's rule: fit parabolas — far more accurate
  • This is what computers actually do
  (pen marks: 8 )

     0: Here's a secret: many integrals have no formula at all.
     1: The integral of e to the minus x squared, which is the bell curve from probability, can't be written with ordinary functions.
  beat1:
     0: So we compute it numerically.
     1: The trapezoid rule splits the area into slices and connects the tops with straight lines.
     2: With just four slices, we get point seven four two nine eight.
  beat2:
     0: Simpson's rule fits little parabolas instead of straight lines.
     1: With the same four slices, it gets point seven four six eight six.
     2: The true value is point seven four six eight two, so Simpson is already correct to four decimal places.
  beat3:

     1: Not with clever antiderivatives, but with smart sums.

[s17] V  Infinite sums can be finite
  img: fig:zeno / a:bouncing_ball
  • 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + … = 1
  • Geometric series: a + ar + ar² + … = a / (1 − r) if |r| < 1
  • Ball dropped 10 m, bounces to 60%: total distance = 10 + 2·(6/0.4) = 40 m
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Look at this square.
     1: Shade half of it.
     2: Then half of what's left, a quarter.
     3: Then an eighth, a sixteenth, and so on forever.
     4: You never run out of square, and you never go past it either.
     5: So one half plus one quarter plus one eighth, forever, equals exactly one.
  beat1:
     0: That's a geometric series, where each term is the previous one times a fixed ratio r.
     1: If the ratio is between negative one and one, the sum is the first term divided by one minus r.
     2: Here that's one half over one minus one half, which is one.
     3: Notice how much easier the formula is than adding forever.
  beat2:
     0: Real example.
     1: Drop a ball from ten meters, and each bounce reaches sixty percent of the previous height.
     2: The total distance is the first ten meters down, plus twice each bounce height, up and down.
     3: The bounces form a geometric series: six, three point six, two point one six, and so on, summing to six over point four, which is fifteen.
     4: So the total is ten plus two times fifteen: forty meters, even though the ball bounces infinitely many times.

[s19] V  But some sums blow up — testing convergence
  img: fig:harmonic / fig:tests
  • Terms shrinking to 0 is NOT enough
  • Harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … diverges
  • Use convergence tests
  (pen marks: 10 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's the trap.
     1: The harmonic series, one plus one half plus one third plus one fourth and so on, has terms that shrink to zero.
     2: Yet the sum grows forever.
     3: Slowly, like the natural log of n: a hundred terms gives about five point two, a thousand terms gives about seven point five, but it never stops growing.
     4: Meanwhile, one plus a quarter plus a ninth, the sum of one over n squared, settles down to pi squared over six.
     5: A famous and surprising result.
  beat1:
     0: So how do you know whether a series converges?
     1: We use tests.
     2: If the terms don't go to zero, it diverges, period.
     3: Geometric series converge when the ratio is less than one.
     4: P series, one over n to the p, converge when p is greater than one.
     5: The ratio test compares each term to the one before, so for n over two to the n the ratio heads to one half, and it converges.
     6: Comparison tests bound your series by one you already know.
     7: And alternating series, with signs flipping and terms shrinking, converge too.
     8: For example, one minus a half plus a third minus a quarter equals the natural log of two.

[s21] V  Power series: a polynomial that never ends

     2: Then the series is a function.
     3: Take the geometric series one plus x plus x squared plus x cubed and so on.

  beat2:

[s23] WB  The Taylor formula: where the factorials come from
  step0 board+: $f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \frac || n-th term:  $\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}\,(x-a)^n$
     0: Let's write the recipe as a formula, because it's on every exam.
     1: Around a point a, f of x equals f of a, plus f prime of a times x minus a, plus f double prime of a over two factorial, times x minus a squared, and so on.
     2: The n th term uses the n th derivative, divided by n factorial.
  step1 board+: $x^3 \to 3x^2 \to 6x \to 6 = 3!$ || PEN ('circle',) on 6 = 3!$ || divide by n! so the derivatives match
     0: Why factorials?
     1: Differentiate x cubed three times: three x squared, then six x, then six, which is three factorial.

  • Put iθ into the series for eˣ
  • Real terms → cos θ, imaginary terms → sin θ
  • e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ
  • A rotating arrow = a sine wave → phasors

     0: Now something remarkable.

  beat1:
     0: The powers of i cycle: i, negative one, negative i, one.
     1: Sort the terms, and all the real ones form exactly the series for cosine theta, and all the imaginary ones form the series for sine theta.
