[s1] V  Semiconductors and the pn junction
  img: fig:doping
  • Pure silicon: a poor conductor
  • Doping: add phosphorus → n-type (electrons), boron → p-type (holes)
  • Junction: carriers diffuse, leaving a depletion region
  • Built-in barrier ≈ 0.7 V in silicon
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Pure silicon is a poor conductor.
     1: Its electrons are locked in bonds, and only a few break free at room temperature, thanks to the band gap you met in physics.
  beat1:
     0: Doping changes that.
     1: Add a tiny amount of phosphorus, which has one extra electron, and you get n type silicon with free electrons.
     2: Add boron, which is missing one, and you get p type silicon, with holes: missing electrons that move like positive charges.
  beat2:
     0: Put p and n regions side by side and something interesting happens.
     1: Electrons near the junction drift across and fill holes, leaving a thin region with no free carriers at all, the depletion region.
  beat3:
     0: The fixed charges left behind create a built in voltage barrier, about point seven volts in silicon.
     1: Every diode, transistor and solar cell is built around junctions like this.

[s2] V  The diode: a one-way valve
  img: fig:diode_iv
  • Forward bias: barrier lowered → current flows (≈ 0.7 V drop)
  • Reverse bias: barrier raised → almost no current
  • Shockley: I = I_S(e^(V/V_T) − 1), V_T ≈ 26 mV
  • Every +60 mV → 10× more current
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: A pn junction makes a diode, which lets current flow one way only.
     1: Apply a positive voltage to the p side, forward bias, and the barrier shrinks, so current flows.
  beat1:
     0: Reverse the voltage, and the barrier grows, so only a tiny leakage current flows.
  beat2:
     0: The Shockley equation describes it: current grows exponentially with voltage, with the thermal voltage, about twenty six millivolts at room temperature, setting the scale.
  beat3:
     0: Exponential is the key word.
     1: Every extra sixty millivolts or so multiplies the current by ten.
     2: That's why, in practice, we treat a conducting silicon diode as having a roughly constant point seven volt drop: the voltage barely changes while the current changes enormously.

[s3] V  Solving diode circuits: the load line
  img: fig:loadline
  • Two equations: the diode curve + the circuit (KVL)
  • Circuit: I = (5 V − V) / 1 kΩ → a straight line
  • Intersection = operating point (Q-point)
  • Quick model: assume 0.7 V → I = 4.3 mA
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Since the diode is nonlinear, we can't just use Ohm's law.
     1: Take a five volt source, a one k resistor and a diode in series.
     2: Two things must both be true: the diode's own curve, and Kirchhoff's voltage law for the circuit.
  beat1:
     0: The circuit equation says the current equals five volts minus the diode voltage, divided by one k.
     1: That's a straight line on the same axes, called the load line.
  beat2:
     0: Where the two cross is the only point satisfying both: the operating point, or Q point.
     1: Here, about point six eight volts and four point three milliamps.
  beat3:
     0: Engineers usually skip the graph and use the constant drop model: assume point seven volts, so the current is four point three volts over one k, four point three milliamps.
     1: Same answer, in your head.
     2: Graphical load line analysis, though, is exactly how you understand transistors too.

[s5] V  Power supplies: rectifiers and ripple
  img: a:bridge_rect / fig:rectifier
  • Bridge of 4 diodes: flips negative halves up
  • Filter capacitor holds the peak between pulses
  • Ripple ≈ V_p / (2 f R C) for full-wave
  • Then a regulator gives clean DC
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Every device that plugs into the wall needs to turn AC into DC.
     1: The first step is a rectifier.
     2: A bridge of four diodes, like the one in this package, steers current so that both halves of the AC wave come out positive.
  beat1:
     0: That bumpy output isn't DC yet, so we add a big filter capacitor.
     1: It charges up to the peak, then slowly discharges into the load between peaks, leaving a small sawtooth called ripple.
     2: For a full wave rectifier, the ripple is about the peak voltage divided by two times the frequency times R times C.
     3: A bigger capacitor or a lighter load means less ripple.
     4: Finally, a voltage regulator, often built from a Zener diode or a dedicated chip, smooths out what's left.
     5: Modern phone chargers use high frequency switching converters instead, but the rectifier and filter capacitor are still right there at the input.

[s7] V  The MOSFET: a voltage-controlled switch
  img: fig:mosfet / fig:mos_iv
  • Gate voltage creates a conducting channel
  • Gate draws no DC current (insulated)
  • Triode: acts like a resistor/switch ON
  • Saturation: I_D = ½ k (V_GS − V_t)² — an amplifier
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: The MOSFET is the transistor inside every digital chip.
     1: Here's an N channel MOSFET in cross section.
     2: Two heavily doped n regions, the source and drain, sit in p type silicon.
     3: Above the gap between them is the gate, separated by a very thin insulating oxide.
     4: Apply a positive gate voltage above the threshold, and it attracts electrons up to the surface, forming a conducting channel from source to drain.
     5: No gate voltage, no channel, no current.
     6: And because the gate is insulated, it draws essentially no DC current at all.
  beat1:
     0: These are its output curves.
     1: At small drain voltages, in the triode region, the channel acts like a resistor whose value is set by the gate voltage.
     2: That's the switch on state used in logic.
     3: At larger drain voltages, the current levels off, in the saturation region, at one half k times the overdrive voltage squared.
     4: There, the gate voltage controls the current almost independently of the drain voltage, like a voltage controlled current source.
     5: That's the amplifier region.
     6: Logic uses the transistor as a switch.
     7: Analog circuits use it as an amplifier.

[s8] V  Driving loads from a microcontroller pin
  img: fig:switch

  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's the transistor circuit computer engineers build most often.
     1: A microcontroller pin can only supply a few milliamps at three point three volts.
     2: That's fine for one small L E D, but not for a motor, a relay or an L E D strip.
  beat1:
     0: So the pin drives the gate of an N channel MOSFET, which switches the load's ground connection.
     1: This is called a low side switch.
     2: The load has its own power supply, here five volts.
  beat2:
     0: When the pin goes high, the transistor turns on and the load runs.
     1: When the pin goes low, it turns off.
     2: The MOSFET's gate draws almost no current, so the microcontroller barely notices.
     3: Choose a logic level MOSFET, one that turns fully on at three point three volts.
  beat3:
     0: One warning for motors, solenoids and relays: they're inductors, and when you switch them off, their current can't stop instantly, so the voltage spikes.
     1: Put a diode backwards across the load, a flyback diode, to give that current a safe path, or it can destroy the transistor.

[s10] V  The BJT and the common-emitter amplifier
  img: a:transistors / fig:ce / fig:ce_wave
  • BJT: base current controls collector current, i_C = β i_B
  • Active region: V_BE ≈ 0.7 V
  • Divider bias + emitter resistor → stable Q-point
  • Gain ≈ −R_C / R_E = −4.7
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:

     1: Transistors come in many packages, from tiny surface mount parts to big metal cans for high power.
     2: In a B J T, a small base current controls a much larger collector current: i C equals beta times i B, where beta is typically around one hundred.
     3: In the active region, the base emitter junction is forward biased, at about point seven volts.
  beat1:
     0: Here's the classic common emitter amplifier.
     1: The resistor divider R one and R two sets the base voltage at about two point one volts, so the emitter sits near one point four volts, and with a one k emitter resistor that sets about one point four milliamps of collector current.
     2: This four resistor bias network is popular because the current depends on the resistors, not on beta, which varies a lot from part to part.
     3: The input capacitor blocks DC, so the signal rides on top of the bias.
  beat2:
     0: With the emitter resistor in place, the voltage gain is about minus R C over R E, minus four point seven here.
     1: A twenty millivolt input becomes about ninety four millivolts at the output, inverted.
     2: Notice the trade: removing R E's effect with a bypass capacitor would raise the gain a lot, but the gain would then depend on temperature and the transistor.
     3: Engineers trade gain for stability all the time.

[s12] V  Small-signal analysis and amplifier types
  img: fig:smallsig
  • Bias at a DC Q-point, then linearize around it
  • g_m = I_C / V_T: 1.4 mA → 54 mS
  • Common emitter/source: gain, inverting
  • Follower (common collector/drain): gain ≈ 1, a buffer
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: To analyze amplifiers, we use the linear approximation from Calculus one.
     1: Set a DC bias point, then treat the transistor's response to tiny signals as a straight line: the tangent line at the Q point.
  beat1:
     0: The slope is the transconductance, g m: the change in output current per change in input voltage.
     1: For a B J T, it's the collector current over the thermal voltage.
     2: At one point four milliamps, that's about fifty four milli siemens.
     3: We replace the transistor with a small signal model and use ordinary circuit analysis.
  beat2:
     0: Three basic configurations.
     1: Common emitter, or common source for a MOSFET, provides voltage gain and inverts the signal.
     2: It's the main amplifier stage.
  beat3:
     0: The emitter follower, or source follower, has a gain of about one, but a high input resistance and low output resistance, so it's a buffer: it can drive a heavy load without loading down the stage before it.
     1: The third type, common base, is used at high frequencies.
     2: Real amplifiers chain these stages together.

[s13] V  Frequency response: amplifiers have a bandwidth
  img: fig:bode
  • Coupling capacitors block very low frequencies
  • Internal capacitances short out high frequencies
  • Miller effect: C_μ multiplied by (1 + gain)
  • Bode plot: gain in dB vs log frequency
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: No amplifier works at every frequency.
     1: At low frequencies, the coupling and bypass capacitors start to block the signal, setting a lower corner frequency.
  beat1:
     0: At high frequencies, tiny capacitances inside the transistor itself start shorting the signal away, setting an upper corner.
  beat2:
     0: In a common emitter stage, one capacitance, from base to collector, is especially harmful.
     1: Because the output swings opposite to the input and much larger, that capacitance appears at the input multiplied by one plus the gain.
     2: That's the Miller effect, and it's often what limits the bandwidth.
  beat3:
     0: We plot this on a Bode plot: gain in decibels against frequency on a log axis.
     1: The corners are where the gain drops by three decibels, half power.
     2: Here, the midband gain is forty decibels, which is a hundred times, from fifty hertz to two hundred kilohertz.
     3: You'll use Bode plots constantly in signals and systems.

[s14] V  Real op amps: gain–bandwidth and slew rate
  img: fig:gbw
  • Gain × bandwidth ≈ constant (GBW)
  • 1 MHz op amp at gain 100 → only 10 kHz
  • Slew rate limits how fast the output can swing
  • Offsets, bias currents, output-swing limits
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Op amps from the circuits course are built from transistors, so they inherit these limits.
     1: The most important one: the product of closed loop gain and bandwidth is roughly constant, called the gain bandwidth product.
  beat1:
     0: An op amp with a one megahertz gain bandwidth product, set to a gain of ten, works up to about one hundred kilohertz.
     1: Set it to a gain of one hundred, and it only works up to ten kilohertz.
     2: If you need both high gain and wide bandwidth, use two stages.
  beat2:
     0: There's also a slew rate: the fastest the output can change, in volts per microsecond.
     1: Ask for a big, fast swing, and the output turns into a triangle wave instead of a sine wave.
  beat3:
     0: And real op amps have small input offset voltages, input bias currents, and outputs that can't quite reach the supply rails, unless you choose a rail to rail part.
     1: Reading these specs on a datasheet is a core analog design skill.
