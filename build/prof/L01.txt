     2: There's literally a hole in the graph at zero.
  beat1:
     0: But watch what happens as x gets close to zero.
     1: At x equals point one, the value is point nine nine eight three.

[s5] V  Computing limits: fix the zero-over-zero
  img: fig:hole
  • Plug in first. If you get a number, done
  • If you get 0/0: factor, cancel, try again
  • (x² − 4)/(x − 2) = (x − 2)(x + 2)/(x − 2) = x + 2 → 4
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: How do you actually compute a limit?
     1: Step one: just plug the number in.
     2: If the function is well behaved there, you get a number, and that's the limit.
  beat1:
     0: If you get zero over zero, the function is hiding something.
     1: Usually a common factor in the top and bottom.
  beat2:
     0: Example: x squared minus four, over x minus two, as x goes to two.
     1: Plugging in gives zero over zero.
     2: But x squared minus four factors into x minus two times x plus two.
     3: Cancel the x minus two, and you're left with x plus two, which is four when x is two.
     4: So the limit is four.
     5: The graph is just the line y equals x plus two, with a single hole punched out at the point two comma four.

[s7] V  Continuity: no holes, jumps, or blow-ups
  img: fig:cont / fig:ivt
  • Continuous at a: f(a) exists, the limit exists, and they match
  • Intermediate Value Theorem: a continuous function crossing from − to + must hit 0
  • Bisection: halve the interval again and again
  (pen marks: 11 )
  beat0:
     0: A function is continuous at a point if you can draw through it without lifting your pen.
     1: Formally, three things must be true: f of a exists, the limit exists, and they're equal.
     2: These three pictures show the three ways that fails: a hole, where the point is missing or misplaced, a jump, and an infinite blow up called an asymptote.
  beat1:
     0: Continuous functions have a very useful property called the intermediate value theorem.
     1: Look at f of x equals x cubed minus x minus one.
     2: At x equals one it's negative one.
     3: At x equals two it's positive five.
     4: Since it's continuous, it must cross zero somewhere in between.
     5: There has to be a root.
  beat2:
     0: And that gives us an algorithm that computers actually use: bisection.
     1: Check the midpoint, one point five.
     2: It's positive, so the root is between one and one point five.
     3: Check one point two five, it's negative, so the root is between one point two five and one point five.
     4: Each step cuts the interval in half, and after about twenty steps you know the root, one point three two four seven, to six decimal places.
     5: That's math turning directly into code.

[s9] V  The derivative: zooming in until a curve looks straight
  img: fig:secant0 / fig:secant1 / fig:secant2 / fig:secant3
  (pen marks: 9 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's the problem.
     1: Slope is rise over run, and that needs two points.
     2: But we want the slope of the curve y equals x squared at one point, x equals three.
     3: So we cheat: take a second point a distance h away, and draw the line through both.
     4: That's called a secant line.
     5: With h equals two, the second point is at five, twenty five, so the slope is twenty five minus nine, over two: eight.
  beat1:
     0: Move the second point closer.
     1: With h equals one, the slope is seven.
  beat2:
     0: With h equals one half, it's six and a half.
     1: Do the algebra in general: the slope is three plus h, all squared, minus nine, all over h.
     2: Expand, and you get six h plus h squared, over h, which is six plus h.
  beat3:
     0: Now let h shrink to zero.
     1: That's a limit!
     2: Six plus h heads to six.
     3: So the slope of the tangent line at x equals three is exactly six.
     4: That's the derivative: f prime of x is the limit, as h goes to zero, of f of x plus h minus f of x, over h.
     5: It's the instantaneous rate of change.
     6: Notice we needed limits, because plugging in h equals zero directly gives zero over zero.

[s10] WB  Worked example: a derivative from the definition
  step0 board+: $f(x)=\frac{1}{x}$ || $f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ || $=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac{1}{x}}{h}$ || PEN None
     0: Let's use the definition ourselves, on a new function: f of x equals one over x.
     1: Write the definition first: the limit as h goes to zero of f of x plus h, minus f of x, over h.
     2: Substitute our function: one over x plus h, minus one over x, all over h.
  step1 board+: $=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{x-(x+h)}{x\,(x+h)\,h}$ || $=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{-h}{x\,(x+h)\,h}$ || PEN ('circle',) on \frac{-h}
     0: Combine the two fractions on top over a common denominator, x times x plus h.
     1: The numerator becomes x minus x plus h, which is just negative h.
     2: So the whole thing is negative h over x times x plus h times h.
  step2 board+: $=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{-1}{x\,(x+h)}$ || $f'(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2}$ || $f'(2) = -\frac{1}{4}$ || PEN None
     0: The h's cancel, which is exactly what removes the zero over zero.
     1: Now let h go to zero: we get negative one over x squared.
     2: At x equals two, the slope is negative one quarter, and you can see that tangent line on the graph, gently sloping downward.

[s11] V  Shortcut rules (so you never use the limit by hand again)
  img: fig:rules
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Computing limits every time would be painful, so we derive rules once and reuse them.
     1: The power rule: the derivative of x to the n is n times x to the n minus one.
     2: So x to the fifth becomes five x to the fourth, and our x squared becomes two x, which is six at x equals three, matching what we found.
     3: Constants just come along for the ride.
     4: Derivatives of sums are sums of derivatives.
     5: Products need the product rule: derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second.
     6: Quotients have the quotient rule: low d high minus high d low, over low squared.
     7: And the most important one, the chain rule, for functions inside functions.
     8: Let's look at that one more closely.

[s12] V  The chain rule: rates multiply
  img: fig:chain
  • Function inside a function: y = sin(3x²)
  • Inner rate × outer rate
  • Gears: if A turns B 3× as fast, and B turns C 2× as fast, C turns 6× as fast as A
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: The chain rule handles composition, a function inside another function.
     1: Take y equals sine of three x squared.
     2: There's an inner function, u equals three x squared, and an outer function, y equals sine of u.
  beat1:
     0: Each stage has its own rate.
     1: The inner one changes at six x.
     2: The outer one changes at cosine of u.
     3: The chain rule says: multiply them.
     4: So d y d x equals cosine of three x squared, times six x.
  beat2:
     0: Think of gears.
     1: If gear A turns gear B three times as fast, and B turns C twice as fast, then C turns six times as fast as A.
     2: Rates multiply through the chain.
     3: You'll use this constantly, including in machine learning, where training a neural network is the chain rule applied millions of times.
     4: That algorithm is called backpropagation.

[s17] V  Linear approximation: the tangent line as a shortcut
  img: fig:linapprox
  • Near a point, a curve ≈ its tangent line
  • f(x) ≈ f(a) + f′(a)(x − a)
  • √4.1 ≈ 2 + ¼(0.1) = 2.025 (true 2.02485)
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Zoom in far enough on any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line, its tangent line.
     1: So near a point, we can replace a complicated function with a simple line.
  beat1:
     0: The formula: f of x is approximately f of a, plus f prime of a times x minus a.
  beat2:
     0: Example: estimate the square root of four point one without a calculator.
     1: We know the square root of four is two, and the derivative of root x is one over two root x, which is one quarter at x equals four.
     2: So root four point one is about two plus one quarter of point one, which is two point zero two five.
     3: The true value is two point zero two four eight five.
     4: Very close!
     5: Engineers use this idea constantly.

[s18] V  Related rates: the sliding ladder
  img: fig:ladder
  • Always true: x² + y² = 100
  • Differentiate in time: 2x·x′ + 2y·y′ = 0
  • y′ = −x·x′ / y = −6·1 / 8
  • **y′ = −0.75 ft/s** (top slides down)
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Related rates problems connect how fast different quantities change.
     1: A ten foot ladder leans against a wall, and the bottom slides away at one foot per second.
     2: How fast is the top sliding down when the bottom is six feet from the wall?
     3: First, write an equation that's always true: x squared plus y squared equals one hundred, by Pythagoras.
     4: Then differentiate both sides with respect to time, using the chain rule: two x times d x d t, plus two y times d y d t, equals zero.
     5: When x is six, y is eight.
     6: Plug in: d y d t equals negative x times d x d t over y, which is negative six over eight, or negative point seven five feet per second.
     7: The negative sign means the top is moving down.
     8: The recipe always works: relate the quantities, differentiate with respect to time, then plug in numbers last.

[s23] V  Newton's method: how calculators find roots
  img: fig:newton

  (pen marks: 8 )
  beat0:

     2: Start with a guess, follow the tangent line down to where it hits zero, and use that as your next guess.
  beat1:
     0: The formula is: the next x equals the current x minus f of x over f prime of x.
  beat2:
     0: To find root two, solve x squared minus two equals zero.
     1: Start at one.
     2: The next guess is one minus negative one over two, which is one point five.
     3: Then one point four one six six seven.
     4: Then one point four one four two one six, which is already correct to six digits.
     5: Each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits.
     6: Bisection, from earlier, is slower but never fails.

[s26] V  The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
