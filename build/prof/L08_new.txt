[s1] V  Vectors in 3D: the dot product
  img: fig:dot
  • a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃ = |a||b| cos θ
  • Zero → perpendicular
  • (1,2,2)·(2,0,1) = 4 → θ ≈ 53.4°
  • 3D graphics: brightness = normal · light direction
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Vectors now have three components.
     1: The dot product multiplies matching components and adds them.
     2: Geometrically, it equals the lengths times the cosine of the angle between them.
  beat1:
     0: So if the dot product is zero, the vectors are perpendicular.
     1: It also gives the projection: how much of one vector points along another.
  beat2:
     0: Example: one, two, two dotted with two, zero, one is two plus zero plus two, which is four.
     1: The lengths are three and root five, so the cosine of the angle is four over three root five, about point six, and the angle is about fifty three degrees.
  beat3:
     0: Every video game uses this billions of times per second.
     1: The brightness of a surface depends on the dot product between its normal vector and the direction to the light.
     2: Facing the light, it's bright.
     3: Turned edge on, the dot product is zero, and it's dark.

[s3] V  The cross product: a perpendicular vector
  img: fig:cross
  • a × b is perpendicular to both a and b
  • Length = |a||b| sin θ = parallelogram area
  • Direction: right-hand rule
  • Torque τ = r × F · surface normals in graphics
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: The cross product of two 3D vectors produces a third vector perpendicular to both.
     1: Its length equals the area of the parallelogram they span, and its direction follows the right hand rule: curl your fingers from a to b, and your thumb points along a cross b.
     2: For example, x hat cross y hat is z hat.
     3: Torque from mechanics is really a cross product: r cross F.
     4: In 3D graphics, the normal vector of each triangle in a model is the cross product of two of its edges.
     5: And in electromagnetism, the force on a moving charge is q v cross B.
     6: Same operation, everywhere.

[s6] V  Partial derivatives: slope in one direction
  img: fig:partial
  • ∂f/∂x: change x, hold y fixed
  • ∂f/∂y: change y, hold x fixed
  • f = x²y + 3y → f_x = 2xy, f_y = x² + 3
  • At (1, 2): f_x = 4, f_y = 4
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: On a landscape, the slope depends on which direction you walk.
     1: A partial derivative measures the slope in one coordinate direction.
     2: The partial with respect to x: change x, and treat y as a constant.
  beat1:
     0: The partial with respect to y: change y, and treat x as a constant.
  beat2:
     0: Example: f equals x squared y plus three y.
     1: With respect to x, y is just a constant, so the derivative is two x y.
     2: With respect to y, x squared is a constant multiplier, so it's x squared plus three.
  beat3:
     0: At the point one, two, both partials equal four.
     1: All the single variable rules still work.
     2: You just have to be disciplined about which variables you're holding still.

[s7] V  The gradient: which way is uphill?
  img: fig:gradient
  • ∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y)
  • Points in the direction of steepest ascent
  • Its length = the steepest slope
  • Always perpendicular to contour lines
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Collect the partial derivatives into a vector, and you get the gradient, written with an upside down triangle called nabla.
  beat1:
     0: Here's the key fact: the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent.
     1: If you're standing on a hillside in the fog, the gradient tells you which way is straight uphill.
  beat2:
     0: Its length tells you how steep that steepest direction is.
     1: The slope in any other direction, called the directional derivative, is the gradient dotted with a unit vector in that direction.
  beat3:
     0: And the gradient is always perpendicular to the contour lines.
     1: Look at the red arrows.
     2: They cross every contour at a right angle, pointing toward the peaks.
     3: Water flows in the opposite direction, straight downhill, which is why streams cross contour lines at right angles on a topographic map.

[s8] V  Gradient descent: how machines learn
  img: fig:gd
  • Want the minimum of an error function?
  • Step opposite the gradient: x ← x − η∇f
  • Repeat until the slope is ~0
  • Neural networks: the same idea with billions of variables
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Now an application that runs much of modern technology.
     1: Suppose you want the lowest point of a function, like the error of a prediction model.
  beat1:
     0: Start anywhere, compute the gradient, and take a small step in the opposite direction, downhill.
     1: The step size, eta, is called the learning rate.
  beat2:
     0: Repeat.
     1: Here, on the bowl f equals x squared plus three y squared, the orange path zigzags down into the minimum at the center.
     2: It zigzags because the bowl is steeper in y than in x.
  beat3:
     0: This is gradient descent, and it's how neural networks are trained.
     1: The error is a function of millions or billions of parameters, the gradient is computed with the chain rule, and the computer takes millions of small downhill steps.
     2: Every image recognizer and language model you've used was trained this way, using exactly this Calculus three idea.

[s10] V  Finding maxima and minima
  img: fig:saddle / fig:lagrange
  • Critical point: ∇f = 0
  • Second-derivative test: max, min, or saddle
  • With a constraint g = c: ∇f = λ∇g (Lagrange)
  • Max xy with x + y = 10 → x = y = 5
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: To find peaks and valleys, look for critical points where the gradient is zero: the surface is flat in every direction.
     1: But flat doesn't always mean a peak or a valley.
     2: Look at the third surface.
     3: It's a saddle: it curves up in one direction and down in the other, like a mountain pass.
     4: A second derivative test, using all the second partial derivatives, tells the three cases apart.
  beat1:
     0: Often you're optimizing with a constraint.
     1: Maximize the area x y of a rectangle, given that x plus y must equal ten, say because you have twenty meters of fence.
     2: Lagrange's insight: at the best point, the contour line of f just touches the constraint curve, so their gradients are parallel.
     3: Gradient of f equals lambda times gradient of g.
     4: Solving gives x equals y equals five, a square, with area twenty five.
     5: Engineers use this to maximize performance subject to power, cost or size limits.

[s12] V  Double integrals: volume under a surface
  img: fig:double
  • ∬ f dA adds f over an area
  • Compute as two nested single integrals
  • ∫₀² ∫₀¹ (x + y) dy dx = ∫₀² (x + ½) dx = 3
  • Mass from density, total charge, average temperature
  (pen marks: 2 )
  beat0:
     0: A double integral adds up a function over a two dimensional region, just like a single integral adds up over an interval.
     1: Geometrically, it's the volume under the surface, made of thin columns, like these bars.
  beat1:
     0: You compute it as two nested single integrals: integrate over y with x held fixed, then integrate the result over x.
  beat2:
     0: Example: x plus y over the rectangle from zero to two in x and zero to one in y.
     1: The inner integral over y gives x plus one half.
     2: Then integrate from zero to two: two plus one, which is three.
     3: When the region isn't a rectangle, the inner limits become functions of the outer variable.
     4: Over the triangle under the line y equals x, for example, y runs from zero up to x, and then x runs over its range.
     5: Always draw the region first, then read the limits off the picture.
  beat3:
     0: Double integrals compute total mass from a density, total charge on a surface, the average temperature over a chip, or the probability that two random variables land in a region, which you'll do in the statistics course.

[s13] V  Change of coordinates: polar, cylindrical, spherical
  img: fig:polar
  • Polar: dA = r dr dθ (the extra r matters!)
  • Gaussian integral: ∫ e^(−x²) dx = √π
  • Spherical: dV = ρ² sin φ dρ dφ dθ
  • Sphere volume (4/3)πr³ falls right out
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Circular regions are painful in x and y, so we switch coordinates.
     1: In polar coordinates, a tiny area element is r d r d theta.
     2: That extra r is crucial: the little patches get bigger as you move outward.
  beat1:
     0: Here's a famous trick.
     1: The integral of e to the minus x squared has no antiderivative.
     2: But square it, turning it into a double integral over the whole plane, and switch to polar.
     3: The extra r makes it easy: the answer is pi.
     4: Take the square root, and the integral of e to the minus x squared is root pi.
     5: That's why root pi appears in the normal distribution's formula.
  beat2:
     0: In three dimensions, cylindrical and spherical coordinates do the same job, with their own volume elements.
     1: In spherical coordinates, it's rho squared sine phi.
  beat3:
     0: Integrate one over a ball in spherical coordinates, and the volume of a sphere, four thirds pi r cubed, falls right out in a couple of lines.

[s15] V  Vector fields, divergence and curl
  img: fig:fields
  • Vector field: an arrow at every point (wind, E, B)
  • Divergence ∇·F: is stuff flowing out? (sources)
  • Curl ∇×F: is it swirling? (rotation)
  • Maxwell’s equations are written with these
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: A vector field attaches an arrow to every point in space: wind velocity on a weather map, water flow in a pipe, the electric field around a charge.
  beat1:
     0: Divergence measures whether a field is spreading out from a point, like the electric field of a positive charge on the left.
     1: A positive divergence means a source.
     2: Negative means a sink.
  beat2:
     0: Curl measures how much the field swirls around a point, like the magnetic field circling a wire carrying current, on the right.
  beat3:
     0: These aren't abstract.
     1: Maxwell's equations, the four equations of electricity and magnetism, say: the divergence of E is proportional to charge density, the divergence of B is zero, the curl of E comes from a changing magnetic field, and the curl of B comes from current and a changing electric field.
     2: You'll meet them next year in physics.

[s17] V  Line integrals and the big theorems
  img: fig:theorems
  • Line integral ∫ F·dr: work along a path
  • Conservative fields: only the endpoints matter
  • Green, Stokes, Divergence: inside ⇄ boundary
  • All are cousins of the Fundamental Theorem
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: A line integral adds up a field along a path.
     1: The work done moving through a force field is the integral of F dot d r along the path.
  beat1:
     0: For conservative fields, like gravity, the work depends only on the starting and ending points, not on the route.
     1: That's exactly why we can define potential energy, and in circuits, voltage.
  beat2:
     0: Then come the three great theorems of vector calculus.
     1: Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem and the divergence theorem all say the same thing in different dimensions: adding up a derivative over the inside of a region equals adding up the field over its boundary.
  beat3:
     0: Look at the first row: the fundamental theorem of calculus is the one dimensional version, where the boundary of an interval is just its two endpoints.
     1: The divergence theorem is what turns Maxwell's equations into Gauss's law, which says the electric flux out of any closed surface equals the charge inside.
     2: It's one beautiful idea, from Calculus one all the way to electromagnetism.
