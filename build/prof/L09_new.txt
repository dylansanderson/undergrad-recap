[s2] V  A differential equation describes how something changes
  img: a:coffee / fig:slope
  • Unknown is a function, not a number
  • dT/dt = −k(T − T_room): cools faster when hotter
  • Slope field: the slope at every point
  • Solutions follow the arrows; equilibrium where slope = 0
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: In algebra, you solve for a number.
     1: In a differential equation, you solve for a whole function, given a rule for how it changes.
     2: Take a hot cup of coffee.
     3: Newton's law of cooling says it loses heat at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the room.
  beat1:
     0: Written as an equation: d T d t equals minus k times T minus twenty, for a room at twenty degrees.
     1: Hot coffee cools fast.
     2: Lukewarm coffee cools slowly.
  beat2:
     0: Even before solving it, we can draw it.
     1: A slope field puts a little arrow at every point, showing the slope the equation demands there.
  beat3:
     0: Every solution is a curve that follows the arrows.
     1: Start at ninety degrees, the orange curve, and it bends down toward twenty.
     2: Start below room temperature, the green curve, and it warms up toward twenty.
     3: The flat red line, where the slope is zero, is the equilibrium.
     4: Everything is attracted to it.

[s3] EX  Solving it: separation of variables
  img: fig:coffee
  CODE:
    dT/dt = −0.1 (T − 20)      T(0) = 90 °C
    
    separate:   dT / (T − 20) = −0.1 dt
    integrate:  ln|T − 20| = −0.1 t + C
    solve:      T(t) = 20 + A e^(−0.1 t)
    initial:    90 = 20 + A  →  A = 70
    
    T(t) = 20 + 70 e^(−0.1 t)
    T(10) = 20 + 70/e ≈ 45.8 °C
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: When a differential equation is separable, put everything with T on one side and everything with t on the other.
     1: Integrate both sides: the natural log of T minus twenty equals negative point one t, plus a constant.
     2: Exponentiate, and T equals twenty plus some constant A times e to the minus point one t.
     3: The initial condition, ninety degrees at time zero, gives A equals seventy.
     4: So after ten minutes, the coffee is at twenty plus seventy over e, about forty six degrees.
     5: Notice the shape: an exponential decay toward a final value.
     6: That's exactly the formula for a capacitor charging or discharging through a resistor, which you'll use constantly in circuits.
     7: Same equation, different physics.
     8: The other main technique, for linear first order equations, is the integrating factor, which handles equations with a forcing term like a changing outside temperature or an input voltage.

[s6] V  Systems of equations and row reduction
  img: fig:rref
  • Write as A x = b
  • Row-reduce the augmented matrix
  • Outcomes: one solution, infinitely many, or none
  • Circuit analysis produces exactly these systems
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Many problems give you several linear equations in several unknowns.
     1: We write them compactly as A x equals b, where A is a matrix of coefficients.
  beat1:
     0: To solve, row reduce the augmented matrix: swap rows, scale rows, and add multiples of one row to another, until it's triangular.
     1: Here, subtracting half of row one from row two eliminates x.
     2: Then back substitute: y equals one, so x equals two.
  beat2:
     0: There are only three possible outcomes.
     1: Exactly one solution, infinitely many solutions with free variables, or no solution at all, which shows up as a row saying zero equals something nonzero.
  beat3:
     0: In circuits, nodal analysis writes Kirchhoff's current law at each node, and you get exactly this kind of system.
     1: A circuit with fifty nodes is a fifty by fifty matrix, and circuit simulators like SPICE solve systems like this millions of times.

[s9] V  A matrix is a transformation
  img: fig:transform
  • Multiplying by A moves every point
  • Columns of A = where the basis vectors land
  • det A = area (or volume) scaling factor
  • det A = 0 → space gets squashed → not invertible
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Here's the most useful way to think about a matrix: it's a machine that moves every point in space.
     1: Multiply a vector by A and you get a new vector.
     2: These pictures show the unit square, in blue, transformed into the orange shape.
  beat1:
     0: The columns of A tell you where the basis vectors land.
     1: The first column is where the x direction goes, the second is where the y direction goes.
     2: That's how 3D graphics rotate and scale every point of a model: one matrix multiply per point.
  beat2:
     0: The determinant is the factor by which areas get scaled.
     1: Stretching x by two doubles areas, so the determinant is two.
     2: Rotations and shears keep area the same, so their determinant is one.
  beat3:
     0: And if the determinant is zero, the matrix squashes the plane flat onto a line.
     1: Information is lost, so you can't undo it: the matrix has no inverse.
     2: This connects a dozen facts into one theorem.
     3: A is invertible exactly when the determinant isn't zero, exactly when the columns are independent, exactly when A x equals b always has a unique solution.

  beat1:

  beat2:

[s12] V  Least squares: fitting a line to noisy data
  img: fig:lsq
  • More equations than unknowns → no exact solution
  • Find the closest one: minimize squared error
  • Normal equations: AᵀA x = Aᵀb
  • Calibration, trend lines, machine learning
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Real measurements are noisy.
     1: If you have twelve data points and want the best line through them, you have twelve equations and only two unknowns, the slope and intercept.
     2: There's no exact solution.
  beat1:
     0: So we find the closest one: the line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical gaps, the red dotted lines.
  beat2:
     0: Linear algebra solves this in one step with the normal equations: A transpose A x equals A transpose b.
     1: Geometrically, it projects the data onto the space of possible lines.
  beat3:
     0: Engineers use least squares to calibrate sensors, fit trend lines, estimate positions from GPS signals, and it's the starting point of machine learning.
     1: Linear regression is literally this slide.

[s14] V  Eigenvalues: directions a matrix only stretches
  img: fig:eigen
  • A v = λ v: v keeps its direction, scaled by λ
  • Solve det(A − λI) = 0
  • A = [[2,1],[1,2]] → λ = 3 on (1,1), λ = 1 on (1,−1)
  • Google’s PageRank is an eigenvector
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Most vectors get turned when you multiply by a matrix.
     1: Look at the light blue arrows: their directions change.
     2: But a few special directions only get stretched.
     3: Those are eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue.
  beat1:
     0: To find them, solve the determinant of A minus lambda I equals zero.
     1: For this matrix, that's two minus lambda squared, minus one, equals zero.
  beat2:
     0: So lambda is three or one.
     1: The eigenvector for three is one, one: multiplying gives three, three, the same direction tripled.
     2: The eigenvector for one is one, negative one, which comes back unchanged.
     3: Those are the orange arrows.
  beat3:
     0: Eigenvectors are everywhere.
     1: Google's original PageRank algorithm ranked web pages by finding the main eigenvector of an enormous matrix of links between pages.
     2: Vibration engineers find the eigenvectors of a structure to learn its natural shapes of vibration.
     3: And as we'll see in a moment, eigenvalues decide whether a system is stable.

[s17] V  Second-order equations: springs, suspensions and RLC circuits
  img: fig:damping / a:tacoma
  • a y″ + b y′ + c y = 0 → try y = e^(rt)
  • Real roots: overdamped · repeated: critical
  • Complex α ± iβ: rings at β while decaying at α
  • Car suspensions aim for near-critical damping
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: A mass on a spring with friction, a car's suspension, and a resistor, inductor and capacitor circuit all obey the same equation: a y double prime plus b y prime plus c y equals zero.
     1: Guess y equals e to the r t, and you get the characteristic equation, a r squared plus b r plus c equals zero.
     2: Its roots decide everything.
     3: Two real roots: overdamped.
     4: The system creeps back without oscillating, the orange curve.
     5: A repeated root: critically damped, the fastest return without overshooting, the green curve.
     6: That's what a car's shock absorbers are designed to approach.
     7: Complex roots, alpha plus or minus i beta: underdamped.
     8: It rings at frequency beta while dying off at rate alpha, the blue curve.
     9: Add a forcing term, like a bumpy road or an input voltage, and the full solution is this homogeneous part plus one particular solution.
  beat1:
     0: And when the forcing frequency matches a system's natural frequency, the response can grow huge.
     1: That's resonance.
     2: The famous nineteen forty collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge is often described as resonance, though engineers today attribute it to a related effect called aeroelastic flutter, where the wind fed energy into the bridge's twisting oscillation.
     3: Either way, the lesson is the same: you must know your system's natural modes.
     4: In circuits, resonance is a feature: it's how a radio tunes to one station.

[s20] V  Systems of ODEs: eigenvalues decide stability
  img: fig:phase
  • x′ = A x for a vector of states
  • Solutions built from e^(λt) v for each eigenpair
  • All Re(λ) < 0 → everything decays: STABLE
  • Any Re(λ) > 0 → something blows up: UNSTABLE
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Real systems have many interacting states: positions and velocities, voltages and currents.
     1: We stack them into a vector x and write x prime equals A x.
  beat1:
     0: The solutions are built from e to the lambda t times v, one term for each eigenvalue and eigenvector pair of A.
     1: So the eigenvalues of A control the behavior of the whole system.
  beat2:
     0: If every eigenvalue has a negative real part, every term decays, and the system settles down.
     1: On the left, the eigenvalues are negative one plus or minus two i, so trajectories spiral inward.
     2: That's stable.
  beat3:
     0: If any eigenvalue has a positive real part, some term grows exponentially, and the system runs away, like the trajectories escaping on the right.
     1: This is the bridge to signals and systems and to control engineering, where those eigenvalues are called poles, and the first question about any design, from an amplifier to a drone, is: are all the poles in the left half plane?
