[s1] V  Object detection with Haar cascades
  img: fig:haar / fig:integral
  • Haar feature: bright region minus dark region
  • Integral image: any rectangle sum in 4 lookups
  • Cheap enough for a Raspberry Pi in real time
  • Viola–Jones (2001): the original real-time face detector
  (pen marks: 11 )
  beat0:
     0: One classic, lightweight way to detect objects is the Viola Jones method, published in two thousand one as the first real time face detector, and available in OpenCV as Haar cascades.
     1: It's built from Haar like features: rectangles where you add up the pixels under the white part and subtract the pixels under the black part.
     2: An edge feature responds to a brightness change, a line feature to a thin stripe.
     3: In a twenty four by twenty four pixel window there are around one hundred sixty thousand possible features of different sizes and positions.
  beat1:
     0: Computing all those sums directly would be far too slow, so the method uses a trick called the integral image.
     1: Each entry stores the sum of every pixel above and to the left.
     2: Then the sum of any rectangle, of any size, takes just four lookups: bottom right, minus top right, minus bottom left, plus top left.
     3: Here, the blue two by two block sums to seven: the highlighted entries give fourteen, minus three, minus five, plus one, which is seven.
     4: Four lookups, for a rectangle of any size.
     5: That's what makes Haar features cheap enough to run on a Raspberry Pi in real time.

[s2] WB  Worked example: a rectangle sum in four lookups
  step0 board+: need: sums of rectangles, fast || PEN None
     0: Haar features need the sum of pixels inside rectangles, millions of times per frame.
     1: Here's a tiny four by four image.
  step1 board+: ii(r, c) = sum above and left || PEN None || PEN ('underline',) on sum above and left || ii(2, 2) = 11 || PEN ('circle',) on ii(2, 2) = 11
     0: The integral image stores, at each cell, the sum of everything above and to the left, including the cell itself.
     1: It's built in one pass.
     2: For example, the cell in row two, column two holds one plus two plus one, plus zero plus one plus three, plus two plus one plus zero: eleven.
  step2 board+: PEN None || 11 − 4 − 3 + 1 = 5
     0: Now the blue rectangle, rows one and two, columns one and two.
     1: Take the corner value eleven, subtract the strip above it, four, subtract the strip to its left, three, and add back the overlap we subtracted twice, one.
     2: Eleven minus four minus three plus one: five.
  step3 board+: check: 1 + 3 + 1 + 0 = 5 || PEN ('check',) on check: 1 + 3 + 1 + 0 = 5 || always 4 lookups, any size || PEN ('underline',) on always 4 lookups
     0: Check by adding the blue pixels directly: one plus three plus one plus zero, five.
     1: It matches.
     2: And the cost is four lookups whether the rectangle is two pixels or two hundred thousand.
     3: That's what made real-time detection possible on our small computer.

[s3] V  AdaBoost and the attentional cascade
  img: fig:cascade
  • AdaBoost picks a few strong features from ~160,000
  • Cascade: cheap early stages reject most windows
  • Rates multiply: 0.995¹¹ ≈ 95% detection
  • 0.44¹¹ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ false alarms per window
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Training uses AdaBoost, a machine learning algorithm that picks, out of all those features, the handful that best separate the object from the background, and weights each one.
     1: Each feature alone is a weak classifier, barely better than guessing.
     2: Combined, they form a strong one.
  beat1:
     0: The second idea is the cascade.
     1: Most windows in an image obviously contain nothing, so the first stage uses just a few features to throw them out quickly.
     2: Only windows that pass go on to the next, more thorough stage, and so on.
     3: On average, very little work is spent per window.
  beat2:
     0: Here's where probability returns.
     1: If each of eleven stages keeps at least ninety nine point five percent of real objects, the overall detection rate is point nine nine five to the eleventh, about ninety five percent.
  beat3:
     0: And if each stage lets through at most forty four percent of background windows, the overall false alarm rate is point four four to the eleventh, about one in eight thousand windows.
     1: Those are exactly the kinds of parameters a team sets when training a cascade for a tool like a stapler or a screwdriver.

[s4] V  Data is everything
  img: a:rpi_camv2 / fig:params
  • Positives: the object at many angles, lights, backgrounds
  • Negatives: the SAME environment without the object
  • Skip near-duplicate and blurry frames
  • Hard-negative mining: add false positives back as negatives
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: In practice, the quality of the training data matters more than any algorithm setting.
     1: Positive images should show the object from many angles, distances, lighting conditions and backgrounds.
     2: Negative images should come from the same environment the robot will work in, just without the object, so the detector learns what the desk, the walls and the clutter look like.
     3: A practical trick when collecting data from video: save only every tenth frame to avoid near duplicates, and automatically reject blurry frames using a sharpness measure, the variance of the image's Laplacian.
     4: A typical successful cascade might use around twelve hundred positive and a thousand negative samples.
     5: And the fastest way to improve a detector is hard negative mining: run it, collect the images where it fired falsely, and add them back as negatives.
  beat1:
     0: At run time, three parameters of OpenCV's detect multi scale function control the trade off.
     1: The scale factor sets how finely object sizes are scanned.
     2: Min neighbors requires several overlapping detections to agree, trading misses for fewer false alarms.
     3: Min size ignores tiny boxes.
     4: Exposing them on sliders and tuning while watching the live camera feed is far faster than guessing.

[s6] V  Finding the object: scan and center
  img: fig:scan
  • Sweep the base servo 0° → 180° in small steps
  • Run the detector at each step
  • On a hit: draw the box, center it in the frame
  • Visual servoing: move until the object is centered
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: With a working detector, the arm needs to find the object.
     1: A simple, robust strategy: sweep the base servo across its range in small steps, say three degrees, grabbing a frame and running the detector at each position.
     2: When the stapler cascade fires, stop, draw a labeled box on the display, and adjust the base until the object sits in the center of the image.
     3: Moving until a visual measurement reaches a target is called visual servoing, and it avoids needing a precise camera calibration.
     4: A more advanced approach calibrates the camera, measuring its focal length and lens distortion, and computes a fixed transformation from camera coordinates to arm coordinates, so a pixel position converts directly into a three dimensional target.

[s7] V  Inverse kinematics: where do the joints go?
  img: fig:ik
  • Forward: angles → hand position (trig)
  • Inverse: position → angles (law of cosines)
  • Two solutions: elbow up / elbow down
  • Real arms: Denavit–Hartenberg + 4×4 matrices
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: Forward kinematics asks: given the joint angles, where is the hand?
     1: For a two link arm it's just trigonometry: x equals L one cosine theta one, plus L two cosine of theta one plus theta two, and similarly for y.
  beat1:
     0: The robot needs the opposite: given a target, what angles reach it?
     1: That's inverse kinematics.
     2: For two links, the law of cosines gives the elbow angle directly, and then a couple of arctangents give the shoulder angle.
  beat2:
     0: There are usually two solutions, elbow up and elbow down.
     1: For links of ten and eight centimeters and a target at twelve, six, elbow up puts the shoulder at about sixty three degrees with the elbow bent minus eighty four, while elbow down uses about minus ten and plus eighty four.
     2: The software picks the one that avoids the table and stays within the servos' ranges.
  beat3:
     0: Real arms with five or six joints use Denavit Hartenberg parameters and four by four homogeneous transformation matrices, multiplying one matrix per joint, the linear algebra from math two fifty B in action.

[s9] V  Moving the joints: servos and PID control
  img: a:servo / fig:pid
  • Hobby servo: 1–2 ms pulse every 20 ms sets the angle
  • Inside: a motor + a position sensor + a controller
  • PID: proportional + integral + derivative
  • Move smoothly; big jumps cause current spikes and wobble
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Small arms use hobby servos like this one.
     1: As we saw with timers, a servo reads a pulse every twenty milliseconds: about one millisecond means one end of travel, two milliseconds the other.
     2: Inside is a small motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer that measures the actual angle, and a little controller that drives the motor until the measured angle matches the commanded one.
  beat1:
     0: That controller, and almost every feedback controller in engineering, is a variation of P I D.
     1: The proportional term pushes harder the bigger the error.
     2: Alone, it may leave a steady error or, with high gain, overshoot and oscillate, as the gray and red curves show.
     3: The integral term accumulates past error and removes any steady offset.
     4: The derivative term reacts to how fast the error is changing and damps the overshoot.
     5: Tuned well, the green curve: fast, with little overshoot and no steady error.
     6: In software, command joint moves in small increments rather than big jumps, which keeps the arm smooth and avoids current spikes.

[s10] WB  Worked example: why P alone leaves an error, and I fixes it
  step0 board+: err = target - x || x  += Kp * err            # Kp = 0.5 || x: 0 → 45 → 67.5 → 78.8 → 84.4 → ... → 90
     0: Let's watch a controller step by step instead of trusting the curves.
     1: A joint sits at zero degrees and must reach ninety.
     2: A proportional controller moves it by K p times the error each step.
     3: With K p of one half, it moves half the remaining distance every time.
  step1 board+: x  += Kp * err - 5        # gravity pulls down 5° || balance:  $0.5\,e = 5$  $\Rightarrow$  $e = 10°$,  stuck at  || PEN ('circle',) on stuck at 80°
     0: Now add gravity: the arm's weight drags it down five degrees every step.
     1: The joint settles where the controller's push exactly balances gravity: one half times the error equals five, so the error is ten.
     2: It stops at eighty degrees, forever.
     3: That's steady state error, and turning up K p only shrinks it, never removes it.
  step2 board+: integ += err || x     += Kp * err + Ki * integ - 5 || PEN ('underline',) on Ki * integ || integral grows until e = 0  →  reaches 90°
     0: The integral term adds up the error over time.
     1: While the arm sits ten degrees short, the sum keeps growing, so the push keeps growing, until the arm reaches ninety, where the error is zero and the integral holds just enough push to cancel gravity.
  step3 board+: P = present,  I = past,  D = future
     0: And the derivative term watches how fast the error is shrinking, and eases off before the target to prevent overshoot.
     1: Proportional for the present, integral for the past, derivative for the future.

[s12] V  Integration and testing: where projects really fail
  img: a:robot_arm / fig:brownout / a:rpi_camv2 / a:rpi_cam
  • Problems live at the interfaces
  • Servo current spikes brown out the computer
  • Lighting changes break the detector
  • Integrate one subsystem at a time; keep a demo video
  beat0:
     0: Most capstone problems don't appear inside a subsystem.
     1: They appear when subsystems meet.
  beat1:
     0: The arm moves, the servos draw a surge of current, the supply voltage sags, and the Raspberry Pi reboots.
     1: The fix is a separate, adequately rated servo supply with a shared ground.
  beat2:
     0: The detector works perfectly at home, then fails in the demo room under different lighting.
     1: The fix is training data from varied lighting, and testing early in the real location.
  beat3:
     0: So test each subsystem alone, integrate one at a time, keep a known good version in version control, and record demo videos as you go, so a last minute failure doesn't erase the proof that it worked.
     1: Today, convolutional neural network detectors like YOLO, running on small accelerators, are far more robust than Haar cascades, and would be the natural upgrade for a project like this.
