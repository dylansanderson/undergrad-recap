  img: a:hamilton
  beat0:
     0: Welcome to C P S C three sixty two, foundations of software engineering.
     1: That's Margaret Hamilton in nineteen sixty nine, standing next to printouts of the Apollo guidance software her team at M I T wrote.
     2: She popularized the term software engineering, insisting that building software deserved the same discipline as building bridges.

[s1] V  Why software engineering exists
  img: a:ariane5 / fig:defectcost
  • Most failures: wrong requirements, poor communication, unmanaged change
  • Ariane 5 (1996): reused code overflowed → rocket lost
  • Defects cost far more to fix the later they’re found
  • Build the right thing, reliably, as a team
  beat0:
     0: Most software failures aren't caused by someone forgetting a semicolon.
     1: They come from building the wrong thing, from teams misunderstanding each other, and from changes nobody tracked.
     2: A famous example is the first flight of the Ariane five rocket in nineteen ninety six.
     3: Its guidance code was reused from the smaller Ariane four.
     4: On the new rocket's faster trajectory, a sixty four bit floating point value was converted into a sixteen bit integer and overflowed.
     5: The software shut down, and the rocket broke apart about forty seconds after launch.
     6: The code had been tested, but against the old rocket's requirements.
  beat1:
     0: The economics push the same way.
     1: A mistake caught while writing requirements costs almost nothing to fix.
     2: The same mistake found after release can cost a hundred times more, because by then it's tangled into the design, the code, the tests, the documentation and the users' data.
     3: So software engineering puts heavy effort into the early stages: understanding the problem, communicating clearly, and catching defects as early as possible.

[s2] V  Process models: waterfall vs. agile
  img: fig:waterfall / a:scrum_board
  • Waterfall: phases in order — clear, but rigid
  • Agile/Scrum: 1–4 week sprints, working software each time
  • Roles: Product Owner, Scrum Master, team
  • Backlog, daily stand-up, sprint review, retrospective
  (pen marks: 10 )
  beat0:
     0: A process model describes how a team moves from idea to product.
     1: The classic waterfall model runs each phase once, in order: requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment.
     2: It's easy to plan and works when requirements are stable, as in some aerospace or medical projects.
     3: But if you discover in testing that the requirements were wrong, going back upstream is very expensive.
     4: Agile methods instead deliver in short loops.
     5: In Scrum, the most common one, work happens in sprints of one to four weeks.
     6: Each sprint the team picks items from a prioritized product backlog, builds them, demonstrates working software at a sprint review, and then holds a retrospective to improve how it works.
  beat1:
     0: The product owner decides what matters most, the scrum master removes obstacles, and the team meets briefly every day in a stand up.
     1: Progress is often tracked on a board like this, with columns such as to do, in progress and done.
     2: Kanban uses the same kind of board and limits how much work is in progress at once.
     3: In this course, students work in teams through several sprints to build a complete mobile app, writing the sprint documents along the way.

[s3] V  Requirements: user stories and use cases
  img: fig:story / fig:usecase
  • Functional (what it does) vs non-functional (speed, security, usability)
  • User story: As a… I want… so that…
  • Acceptance criteria make it testable
  • Use case: actor, main flow, alternate flows
  (pen marks: 10 )
  beat0:
     0: Requirements come in two kinds.
     1: Functional requirements say what the system does: log a workout, sign in with Google.
     2: Non functional requirements say how well: it must load in under two seconds, protect personal data, and be usable one handed.
     3: Agile teams capture functional requirements as user stories in a simple template: as a gym member, I want to log a workout in under thirty seconds, so that I keep a streak without breaking my routine.
     4: The so that part matters, because it explains the value.
     5: Acceptance criteria then make the story testable: saving takes at most three taps, it works offline, and the streak updates immediately.
     6: Good stories follow INVEST: independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small and testable.
  beat1:
     0: Use cases add more detail.
     1: A use case diagram shows the actors outside the system, here a member and the Google sign in service, and the goals they accomplish inside it.
     2: Each use case is then written out with preconditions, the main success scenario step by step, and alternate flows for errors, like what happens when there's no network.

[s4] WB  Worked example: turning a wish into a testable requirement
  step0 board+: "The arm should respond quickly." || PEN ('circle',) on quickly || ✗ untestable: how fast? measured how? || PEN ('cross',) on "The arm should respond quickly."
     0: Here's a requirement from a real first draft: the robot arm should respond quickly.
     1: It sounds fine, but it can't be tested.
     2: Quickly compared to what?
     3: Measured how?
     4: Let's fix it.
  step1 board+: "The arm shall begin moving within 500 ms ||  of a recognized voice command, ||  in at least 95 of 100 trials." || PEN ('underline',) on within 500 ms || PEN ('underline',) on 95 of 100 trials
     0: A testable requirement names a measurable quantity, a limit, and how it will be checked.
     1: The arm shall begin moving within five hundred milliseconds of a recognized voice command, in at least ninety five of one hundred trials.
  step2 board+: As a user with limited hand mobility, || I want to say "pick up the cup", || so that I can drink without help.
     0: The same need, written as a user story: as a user with limited hand mobility, I want to say pick up the cup, so that I can drink without help.
     1: The story explains who and why.
  step3 board+: GIVEN the cup is in view and within reach || PEN ('box',) on GIVEN the cup || WHEN the user says "pick up the cup" || THEN the arm grasps it and lifts 5 cm, no spill
     0: And acceptance criteria make the story testable.
     1: Given the cup is within reach and in view, when the user says pick up the cup, then the arm grasps it and lifts it five centimeters without spilling.
     2: At the end of the project, these sentences become your test plan.

[s5] V  UML: class and sequence diagrams
  img: fig:classdiag / fig:sequence
  • Class diagram: classes, attributes, methods, relationships
  • Multiplicity: 1, 0..*, 1..*
  • Composition ◆ · aggregation ◇ · inheritance △
  • Sequence diagram: messages between objects over time
  (pen marks: 12 )
  beat0:
     0: The unified modeling language, UML, is the standard notation for software design.
     1: A class diagram shows classes with their attributes and methods, and how they relate.
     2: Here, one user has zero or more workouts, written one to zero dot dot star.
     3: A workout is composed of one or more exercises, shown with a filled diamond: composition means the exercises don't exist without their workout, so deleting the workout deletes them.
     4: A hollow diamond, aggregation, would mean the parts can live on their own.
     5: And the hollow triangle is inheritance, from object oriented programming: a run is a kind of workout, with its own way of computing calories.
  beat1:
     0: A sequence diagram shows one scenario over time.
     1: Objects run across the top, time flows downward, and arrows are messages.
     2: The member taps sign in, the login screen calls the auth service, which sends an OAuth request to Google, gets a token back, returns the user's profile, and the screen shows the dashboard.
     3: Dashed arrows are returns.
     4: Sequence diagrams are great for finding missing steps before writing code.
     5: Activity diagrams and state machine diagrams, the same finite state machines from digital logic, complete the toolkit.

[s6] WB  Worked example: drawing a class diagram
  step0 board+: nouns: member, book, loan || PEN ('underline',) on nouns: member, book, loan || PEN None || −  private,   +  public
     0: Let's model a library system.
     1: Start with the nouns from the requirements: member, book, and loan.
     2: A Member class has a private name and I D, written with a minus sign, and a public borrow method, written with a plus.
  step1 board+: PEN None
     0: A Book has a title and an I S B N, and can report whether it's available.
  step2 board+: PEN None || member: 0..5 loans || PEN ('circle',) on member: 0..5 loans || book: 0..* loans over time || PEN ('circle',) on book: 0..* loans
     0: A Loan connects one member to one book, with a due date.
     1: Now the multiplicities.
     2: Each member can hold zero to five loans at a time.
     3: Each book can appear in many loans over its lifetime, zero or more.
  step3 board+: rules visible before code
     0: Notice what the diagram just did: the rule maximum five books per member is now visible to everyone, before any code is written.
     1: That's the point of UML: catching design decisions while they're still cheap to change.

[s7] V  Design principles and architecture
  img: fig:coupling / fig:mvc
  • High cohesion: each module does one job
  • Low coupling: modules depend on each other little
  • SOLID, information hiding, separation of concerns
  • Architectures: layered, client–server, MVC/MVVM, microservices
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: Two words summarize good design: cohesion and coupling.
     1: A cohesive module does one well defined job.
     2: Loosely coupled modules know as little about each other as possible, so you can change one without breaking the others.
     3: On the left, everything talks to everything, so any change ripples everywhere.
     4: On the right, the user interface, the logic and the data are separate, with narrow, defined connections.
     5: The SOLID principles, starting with single responsibility, are a checklist for achieving exactly this.
  beat1:
     0: At a larger scale, an architecture organizes the whole system.
     1: Most mobile and web apps follow model view controller or its cousin, model view view model, which Android recommends.
     2: The view only displays and collects input.
     3: The controller or view model handles the user's actions.
     4: The model holds the data and the business rules, and notifies the view when data changes, using the observer pattern.
     5: Design patterns like observer, factory, strategy and adapter are named, reusable solutions to recurring design problems, so a team can say use a strategy here and everyone understands.

[s8] V  Testing and quality
  img: fig:testpyramid
  • Unit → integration → system → acceptance
  • Many fast unit tests, fewer slow end-to-end tests
  • Black-box: equivalence classes, boundary values
  • Regression tests stop old bugs coming back
  (pen marks: 8 )
  beat0:
     0: Testing happens at several levels.
     1: Unit tests check a single function or class in isolation.
     2: Integration tests check that modules work together.
     3: System tests check the whole application, and acceptance tests check that it meets the user's actual needs, often written straight from the acceptance criteria.
     4: The testing pyramid says to write many fast unit tests and fewer slow end to end tests, because fast tests get run constantly and catch problems within minutes.
     5: For choosing test inputs, black box techniques work from the specification.
     6: Equivalence partitioning groups inputs that should behave the same, and boundary value analysis tests the edges, where bugs love to hide.
     7: If ages from thirteen to one hundred twenty are allowed, test twelve, thirteen, one hundred twenty and one hundred twenty one.
     8: White box testing instead looks at the code and aims to cover its branches.
     9: And whenever a bug is fixed, add a regression test, so it can never quietly return.

[s9] WB  Worked example: boundary-value tests catch a real bug
  step0 board+: bool IsValidAge(int age) { ||   return age > 18 && age <= 65; || } || PEN ('circle',) on age > 18
     0: The requirement says: ages eighteen through sixty five are valid.
     1: Here's an implementation.
     2: Look carefully, and see if you can spot the bug before the tests do.
  step1 board+: test: 17, 18, 40, 65, 66 || for (int a : {17, 18, 40, 65, 66}) ||   std::cout << a << ": " << IsValidAge(a) << "\n"; || PEN ('underline',) on test: 17, 18, 40, 65, 66
     0: Bugs cluster at boundaries, so boundary value analysis tests just below, on, and just above each edge: seventeen, eighteen, sixty five and sixty six, plus one ordinary value, forty.
  step2 board+: 17: 0 || 18: 0    <- should be 1! || PEN ('circle',) on 18: 0    <- should be 1! || 40: 1 || 65: 1 || 66: 0
     0: Run it.
     1: Seventeen is invalid: correct.
     2: But eighteen also says invalid!
     3: The code wrote greater than, where the requirement means greater than or equal.
     4: Forty, sixty five and sixty six are all correct.
  step3 board+:   return age >= 18 && age <= 65;   // fixed || PEN ('circle',) on >= 18 || test the edges: 17 | 18 ... 65 | 66 || PEN ('underline',) on test the edges
     0: Change one character, greater than to greater than or equal, rerun, and all five pass.
     1: A random test like twenty five would never have found this.
     2: Testing the edges is the cheapest bug hunting there is.

[s10] V  Version control, code review and CI
  img: fig:gitflow
  • Git: every change recorded, every version recoverable
  • Feature branches keep main working
  • Pull requests: code review before merging
  • Continuous integration runs the tests on every push
  (pen marks: 8 )
  beat0:
     0: Teams can't work on the same code without version control.
     1: Git records every change, who made it and why, and lets you recover any earlier version.
  beat1:
     0: Developers work on feature branches, so the main branch always stays in a working state.
     1: Here, a meal logging feature gets its own branch with several commits.
  beat2:
     0: When it's ready, the developer opens a pull request.
     1: Teammates review the code, ask questions and suggest improvements before it's merged.
     2: Code review catches bugs, and just as importantly, spreads knowledge across the team.
  beat3:
     0: Continuous integration servers automatically build the project and run the whole test suite on every push, so a broken change is flagged within minutes, while it's still fresh in the author's mind.
     1: This is how nearly every professional software team works today.

  step0 board+: $ git switch -c feature/voice-cmd || PEN None || PEN None || PEN ('underline',) on git switch -c

  step1 board+: $ git add speech.py || $ git commit -m "Map 'pick up' to grasp action" || $ git push -u origin feature/voice-cmd

[s12] V  Planning, estimation and risk
  img: a:scrum_board / a:scrum_board / a:scrum_board / fig:risk / fig:risk
  • Break work down: epics → stories → tasks
  • Agile: story points and team velocity
  • Traditional: Gantt charts, critical path, COCOMO
  • Risk table: probability × impact → mitigation plan
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Finally, planning.
     1: Large goals are broken into epics, epics into user stories, and stories into concrete tasks.
  beat1:
     0: Agile teams estimate stories in relative units called story points, then measure velocity, how many points the team actually completes per sprint.
     1: After a few sprints, velocity predicts the future far better than optimism does.
  beat2:
     0: Traditional projects use Gantt charts and critical path analysis to find the chain of tasks that determines the end date, and models like COCOMO estimate effort from the expected size of the code.
  beat3:
     0: And every project keeps a risk table: for each risk, estimate its probability and its impact, multiply them to rank the risks, and plan mitigations for the top ones.
     1: A third party API changing, a key team member getting sick, or an app store review delay are classic risks for a mobile project.
