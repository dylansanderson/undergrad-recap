[s2] V  Vectors: quantities with direction
  img: fig:vectors
  • Scalar: just a size (mass, time, speed)
  • Vector: size + direction (displacement, velocity, force)
  • Add vectors tip-to-tail, or by components
  • 3 km east + 4 km north = 5 km at 53.1°
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: Some quantities only have a size, like mass or temperature.
     1: Those are scalars.
  beat1:
     0: Others have a size and a direction, like displacement, velocity and force.
     1: Those are vectors.
     2: Push a box with fifty newtons, and it matters a lot whether you push left or right.
  beat2:
     0: To add vectors, place them tip to tail, or more practically, break each into x and y components and add the components separately.
  beat3:
     0: Example: walk three kilometers east, then four kilometers north.
     1: Your net displacement has length square root of three squared plus four squared, which is five kilometers, at an angle of inverse tangent of four over three, which is fifty three point one degrees north of east.
     2: Breaking things into components is the single most useful trick in this course.

[s4] V  Kinematics: describing motion
  img: fig:vt
  • v = v₀ + at
  • x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²
  • v² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀)
  • Car 0 → 60 mph (27 m/s) in 6 s: a = 4.5 m/s², 81 m
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Kinematics describes motion without asking what causes it.
     1: For constant acceleration, three equations do almost everything.
     2: Velocity grows linearly: v equals v naught plus a t.
  beat1:
     0: Position follows x equals x naught plus v naught t plus one half a t squared.
     1: That should look familiar from calculus: position is the integral of velocity.
  beat2:
     0: And when you don't know the time: v squared equals v naught squared plus two a times the distance.
  beat3:
     0: Example: a car goes from zero to sixty miles per hour, about twenty seven meters per second, in six seconds.
     1: The acceleration is twenty seven divided by six, four point five meters per second squared.
     2: That's the slope of the velocity graph.
     3: The distance covered is the area under it: one half times six times twenty seven, which is eighty one meters.

[s5] V  Projectile motion: two independent problems
  img: fig:projectile
  • Horizontal: constant velocity
  • Vertical: constant acceleration (−9.8 m/s²)
  • 20 m/s at 45°: range 40.8 m, peak 10.2 m, 2.9 s
  • 30° and 60° land at the same spot
  (pen marks: 9 )
  beat0:
     0: Throw something, and its motion splits into two independent problems.
     1: Horizontally, nothing pushes it, so it moves at constant velocity, ignoring air resistance.
  beat1:
     0: Vertically, gravity accelerates it downward at nine point eight meters per second squared, exactly like the ball we threw straight up in calculus.
  beat2:
     0: Launch at twenty meters per second at forty five degrees.
     1: The vertical speed is twenty times sine forty five, about fourteen point one.
     2: It stays in the air for two times fourteen point one over nine point eight, about two point nine seconds, and in that time travels about forty point eight meters horizontally, peaking at ten point two meters.
  beat3:
     0: Notice that thirty and sixty degrees land at the same spot, about thirty five meters, because the range depends on sine of twice the angle.
     1: Forty five degrees gives the maximum range, at least without air resistance.

[s7] V  Newton's three laws
  img: a:rocket_launch / a:rocket_launch / a:rocket_launch
  • 1. Objects keep doing what they’re doing unless a net force acts
  • 2. F_net = m a
  • 3. Every force has an equal and opposite partner
  beat0:
     0: Now, what causes motion?
     1: Isaac Newton's three laws.
     2: First: an object at rest stays at rest, and an object moving stays moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a net force acts on it.
     3: Motion doesn't need a cause.
     4: Changes in motion do.
  beat1:
     0: Second: the net force equals mass times acceleration.
     1: Double the force, double the acceleration.
     2: Double the mass, half the acceleration.
     3: This one equation is the engine of all of mechanics.
  beat2:
     0: Third: forces come in pairs.
     1: If A pushes on B, B pushes back on A equally hard in the opposite direction.
     2: A rocket pushes hot gas down out of its engines, and the gas pushes the rocket up.
     3: It doesn't need air to push against, which is why rockets work in space.

[s8] V  Free-body diagrams: a box on a ramp
  img: fig:incline
  • Draw every force on ONE object
  • Tilt the axes along the slope
  • Along: 98 sin30° − 0.2 × 84.9 = 32.0 N
  • a = 32.0 / 10 = 3.2 m/s² down the ramp
  (pen marks: 8 )
  beat0:
     0: To use F equals m a, draw a free body diagram: the object alone, with an arrow for every force acting on it.
     1: Here's a ten kilogram box sliding down a thirty degree ramp, with a friction coefficient of point two.
     2: Gravity pulls straight down with m g, ninety eight newtons.
     3: The ramp pushes back perpendicular to its surface with the normal force.
     4: And friction opposes the sliding.
  beat1:
     0: Trick: tilt your axes so x runs along the slope.
     1: Then the normal force and the perpendicular part of gravity cancel: N equals m g cosine thirty, eighty four point nine newtons.
  beat2:
     0: Along the slope, gravity's component is ninety eight times sine thirty, forty nine newtons.
     1: Friction is the coefficient times the normal force: point two times eighty four point nine, about seventeen newtons, pointing up the slope.
     2: The net force is about thirty two newtons down the slope.
  beat3:
     0: Divide by the mass: the box accelerates at about three point two meters per second squared.
     1: Every mechanics problem, from bridges to robot joints, follows this same recipe: isolate the object, draw the forces, pick smart axes, apply F equals m a.

  step0 board+: PEN None || Find a and T (no friction)

     1: A four kilogram block sits on a frictionless table.
     2: A string runs over a pulley at the edge to a two kilogram block hanging below.
     3: What's the acceleration, and what's the tension in the string?
  step1 board+: PEN None || table block:  $T = 4a$ || hanging block:  $2g - T = 2a$ || PEN None
     0: Draw a free body diagram for each block.
     1: The table block feels only the tension T pulling it sideways.
     2: The hanging block feels T pulling up and its weight, two g, pulling down.
     3: The string keeps them moving together, so they share the same acceleration a.
  step2 board+: add:  $2g = 6a$ || $a = \frac{2(9.8)}{6} \approx 3.27\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$
     0: Add the two equations, and the tension cancels: two g equals six a.
     1: So a is two times nine point eight over six, about three point two seven meters per second squared.
  step3 board+: $T = 4a \approx 13.1\ \mathrm{N}$ || check: $T < 2g = 19.6\ \mathrm{N}$
     0: Then the tension is four times a: about thirteen point one newtons.
     1: Sanity check: that's less than the hanging block's weight of nineteen point six newtons, which makes sense, because the hanging block is accelerating downward.
     2: If the tension equaled its weight, nothing would move.

[s10] V  Apparent weight: what your phone’s accelerometer feels
  img: fig:elevator
  • Scale reads the normal force N, not mg
  • N = m(g + a)
  • Going up faster: you feel heavier
  • Free fall: N = 0 — "weightless"
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Stand on a bathroom scale in an elevator.
     1: The scale doesn't measure gravity directly.
     2: It measures how hard it has to push up on you: the normal force.
  beat1:
     0: Apply Newton's second law: N minus m g equals m a, so N equals m times g plus a.
  beat2:
     0: A seventy kilogram person reads six hundred eighty six newtons at rest.
     1: Accelerating upward at two meters per second squared, the reading jumps to eight hundred twenty six newtons.
     2: You feel heavier.
     3: Accelerating downward, it drops to five hundred forty six.
  beat3:
     0: And in free fall, the reading is zero.
     1: That's why astronauts in orbit float: they're falling around the Earth continuously.
     2: An accelerometer works exactly like this scale.
     3: That's why a phone lying still on a table reports about one g pointing up, and how it knows which way is up to rotate your screen.

[s11] V  Circular motion: why cars slide on curves
  img: fig:circular
  • Moving in a circle = constantly changing direction
  • Centripetal acceleration a = v²/r, toward the center
  • Something must supply that force: friction, tension, gravity
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Even at constant speed, moving in a circle means your velocity keeps changing direction.
     1: And a change in velocity is an acceleration.
  beat1:
     0: That centripetal acceleration points toward the center, with size v squared over r.
     1: A car at twenty meters per second, about forty five miles per hour, on a curve of radius fifty meters needs eight meters per second squared toward the center.
  beat2:
     0: Something has to supply that force.
     1: For a car, it's friction between the tires and the road.
     2: The friction coefficient must be at least eight over nine point eight, about point eight two.
     3: Dry pavement can do that.
     4: A wet road, with a coefficient around point five, can't, and the car slides outward.
     5: That's why curve speed limits exist, and why highway curves are banked.
     6: The same equation sizes the spin speed of hard drive platters and the forces in a centrifuge.

[s12] V  Energy: the bookkeeping shortcut
  img: fig:energy
  • Kinetic energy ½mv² · potential energy mgh
  • Without friction, the total never changes
  • Drop 30 m: v = √(2gh) = 24.2 m/s (87 km/h)
  • Work W = F·d changes energy
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Forces and accelerations can get messy.
     1: Energy is often a much faster route.
     2: Moving objects have kinetic energy, one half m v squared.
     3: Raised objects have gravitational potential energy, m g h.
  beat1:
     0: Without friction, energy just converts between these forms, and the total stays constant.
     1: Look at the roller coaster.
     2: At A, it's all potential energy, the blue bar.
     3: At B, the bottom, most of it has become kinetic, orange.
     4: At C, it's partly back to potential.
  beat2:
     0: So how fast is a coaster after dropping thirty meters?
     1: Set m g h equal to one half m v squared.
     2: The mass cancels, and v is the square root of two g h, about twenty four point two meters per second, or eighty seven kilometers per hour.
     3: No forces, no time, no calculus.
     4: Just bookkeeping.
  beat3:
     0: Work, force times distance, is how energy enters or leaves.
     1: Friction does negative work, turning motion into heat.
     2: In electronics, you'll see the same conservation idea: energy delivered by a battery ends up as heat in resistors, light in LEDs, or stored in capacitors.

[s14] V  Momentum and collisions
  img: fig:collision
  • Momentum p = mv; total is conserved in collisions
  • 1000 kg at 20 m/s hits 1500 kg at rest, they stick: 8 m/s
  • Kinetic energy: 200 kJ → 80 kJ (rest crushes metal)
  • Impulse: F·Δt = Δp → airbags stretch Δt, cut the force
  (pen marks: 6 )
  beat0:
     0: Momentum is mass times velocity.
     1: When objects collide, the forces between them are equal and opposite, so the total momentum before equals the total after.
  beat1:
     0: A thousand kilogram car at twenty meters per second hits a fifteen hundred kilogram car at rest, and they stick together.
     1: Momentum before: twenty thousand.
     2: After, two thousand five hundred kilograms share it, so they move at eight meters per second.
  beat2:
     0: But kinetic energy is not conserved here.
     1: It drops from two hundred kilojoules to eighty.
     2: The missing one hundred twenty kilojoules went into crushing metal, heat and sound.
     3: Car crumple zones are designed to absorb exactly that energy.
  beat3:
     0: And impulse explains safety design.
     1: Force times time equals the change in momentum.
     2: Your momentum has to drop to zero in a crash either way.
     3: An airbag stretches the stopping time from a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, so the force on you drops by roughly the same factor.
     4: Electronics borrowed a related trick: many laptops with spinning hard drives used an accelerometer to detect a fall and park the drive's read head before impact.

[s15] V  Rotation: torque
  img: fig:torque / fig:analog
  • Torque τ = r F sin θ: the “twisting force”
  • 0.3 m wrench × 50 N = 15 N·m
  • Every linear idea has a rotational twin
  (pen marks: 10 )
  beat0:
     0: Spinning things are described by rotational versions of everything we've done.
     1: The rotational version of force is torque: how hard you twist.
     2: Torque equals the distance from the pivot times the force times the sine of the angle between them.
     3: Push at the end of a thirty centimeter wrench with fifty newtons, at a right angle, and you apply fifteen newton meters.
     4: Use a longer wrench and you need less force for the same torque.
     5: That's why it's easier to open a door by pushing far from the hinges.
  beat1:
     0: Every linear quantity has a twin.
     1: Position becomes angle.
     2: Velocity becomes angular velocity.
     3: Mass becomes moment of inertia, which depends on how far the mass is from the axis.
     4: F equals m a becomes torque equals I alpha.
     5: And kinetic energy becomes one half I omega squared.
     6: Learn one column, and the other comes almost for free.

[s18] V  Gravity and orbits: how GPS satellites stay up
  img: a:gps_sat / fig:orbit
  • F = G m₁m₂ / r²
  • Orbit: gravity supplies the centripetal force
  • v = √(GM/r) — no fuel needed to stay in orbit
  • GPS: ~26,600 km radius, 3.9 km/s, 12-hour orbit
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Newton's law of gravitation says every two masses attract with a force G m one m two over r squared.
     1: At the Earth's surface, that gives the familiar nine point eight meters per second squared.
  beat1:
     0: Now combine it with circular motion.
     1: A satellite in orbit is falling toward Earth the whole time, but it's moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing.
     2: Gravity provides exactly the centripetal force: G M m over r squared equals m v squared over r.
     3: The satellite's mass cancels, and the orbital speed is the square root of G M over r.
     4: No engine needed to stay up.
     5: GPS satellites like this one orbit at a radius of about twenty six thousand six hundred kilometers, moving at about three point nine kilometers per second, and circle the Earth every twelve hours.
     6: Your phone finds your position by timing signals from several of them, which you'll appreciate more after the electromagnetism and signals courses.
