[s1] V  Sample spaces and the axioms
  img: a:dice / fig:twodice
  • Sample space S: all possible outcomes
  • Event: a subset of S
  • Axioms: P ≥ 0, P(S) = 1, disjoint events add
  • P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A) · P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
  (pen marks: 1 )
  beat0:
     0: Probability starts with a sample space, the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.
     1: Roll two dice, and there are thirty six equally likely outcomes.
     2: An event is any subset of outcomes, like the sum is seven.
  beat1:
     0: Everything follows from three axioms: probabilities are never negative, the whole sample space has probability one, and probabilities of events that can't happen together add up.
     1: From those, we get rules like the complement, one minus P of A, and inclusion exclusion from discrete math.
     2: Six of the thirty six outcomes sum to seven, so the probability of rolling a seven is one sixth, the most likely sum.

[s2] V  Conditional probability and Bayes’ rule
  img: fig:bayes
  • P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
  • Bayes: P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B)
  • 2% defective, test catches 95%, false alarm 10%
  • A flagged chip is bad only ≈ 16% of the time!
  (pen marks: 8 )
  beat0:
     0: Conditional probability is the probability of A given that we know B happened: the probability of both, divided by the probability of B.
     1: Knowing B shrinks the sample space.
  beat1:
     0: Bayes' rule flips a conditional around: the probability of A given B equals the probability of B given A, times the probability of A, divided by the probability of B.
     1: It's how you update a belief after seeing evidence.
  beat2:
     0: Here's an engineering example that surprises almost everyone.
     1: Two percent of chips from a production line are defective.
     2: A quick test catches ninety five percent of bad chips, but it also falsely flags ten percent of good ones.
     3: A chip gets flagged.
     4: What's the chance it's actually bad?
  beat3:
     0: Count it out with a thousand chips.
     1: Twenty are bad, and the test flags nineteen of them.
     2: Nine hundred eighty are good, and the test falsely flags ninety eight.
     3: So a hundred seventeen chips are flagged, and only nineteen are actually bad: about sixteen percent.
     4: When the condition is rare, even a good test produces mostly false alarms.
     5: The same math applies to medical tests, spam filters, and fraud detection.

[s4] V  Independence
  img: fig:reliab
  • Independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B)
  • Knowing B tells you nothing about A
  • NOT the same as mutually exclusive!
  • Reliability: 3 independent parts at 99% → 0.99³ ≈ 97%
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: Two events are independent if the probability of both equals the product of their probabilities.
     1: Knowing that one happened tells you nothing about the other, like two separate dice.
  beat1:
     0: Equivalently, the conditional probability of A given B is just P of A.
  beat2:
     0: A common mistake: independent is not the same as mutually exclusive.
     1: If two events can't happen together and both are possible, then knowing one happened tells you the other definitely didn't.
     2: That's maximum dependence.
  beat3:
     0: Independence makes reliability calculations easy.
     1: A system that works only if all three of its independent components work, each with ninety nine percent reliability, works with probability point nine nine cubed, about ninety seven percent.
     2: Add more components in series and reliability drops quickly.
     3: Put them in parallel, with redundancy, and it rises.

[s5] V  Bernoulli trials and the binomial distribution
  img: fig:binomial
  • n independent trials, success probability p
  • P(k successes) = C(n,k) pᵏ (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ
  • Batch of 20 chips, 5% defect rate → P(no defects) ≈ 36%
  • Large n, small p → Poisson with λ = np
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Repeat an experiment n times independently, each with the same success probability p.
     1: The number of successes follows the binomial distribution.
     2: The formula combines the counting from discrete math, n choose k ways to pick which trials succeed, with the probability of each such sequence.
  beat1:
     0: Example: chips are defective with probability five percent.
     1: In a batch of twenty, what's the probability of zero defects?
     2: Point nine five to the twentieth, about thirty six percent.
     3: Exactly one defect: about thirty eight percent.
     4: The chart shows the full distribution.
  beat2:
     0: When n is large and p is small, the binomial is well approximated by the Poisson distribution, with parameter lambda equals n p.
     1: Poisson describes counts of rare events in an interval: packets arriving per millisecond, photons hitting a sensor pixel, or radiation induced bit flips in memory per day.

[s7] V  Random variables, PDFs and CDFs
  img: fig:pdfcdf
  • Random variable: a number assigned to each outcome
  • CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x): rises from 0 to 1
  • PDF f(x) = dF/dx: area under it = probability
  • Mean E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx · variance σ² = E[X²] − E[X]²
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: A random variable assigns a number to each outcome: the voltage of a noisy signal, the lifetime of a component, the number of errors in a packet.
  beat1:
     0: The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, gives the probability that X is at most x.
     1: It rises from zero to one.
  beat2:
     0: Its derivative is the probability density function, or PDF.
     1: For continuous variables, probability is area under the PDF.
     2: The probability that X lands between a and b is the integral from a to b.
     3: This is where Calculus pays off.
  beat3:
     0: Two numbers summarize a distribution.
     1: The mean, or expected value, is the integral of x times f of x: the center of mass.
     2: The variance measures spread: the expected squared distance from the mean, and its square root is the standard deviation, sigma.

[s8] WB  Worked example: mean and variance, by hand
  step0 board+: die:  $E[X] = \sum x\,p(x) = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = 3.5$ || PEN ('circle',) on = 3.5$

  step2 board+: uniform on $[0,2]$:  $f(x) = \frac{1}{2}$ || $E[X] = \int_0^2 x\cdot\frac{1}{2}\,dx = 1$ || $E[X^2] = \int_0^2 x^2\cdot\frac{1}{2}\,dx = \frac{4}{3}$

  step3 board+: $\sigma^2 = \frac{4}{3} - 1^2 = \frac{1}{3}$,   $\sigma \app || $f(x)$ can exceed 1:  only AREA is probability || PEN ('underline',) on $f(x)$ can exceed 1
     0: Variance is E of X squared minus the mean squared: four thirds minus one, one third.
     1: The standard deviation is its square root, about point five eight.
     2: And one warning: a density can be bigger than one.
     3: Uniform on zero to one half has height two.
     4: Only areas are probabilities.

[s9] V  The Gaussian (normal) distribution
  img: fig:gauss
  • Bell curve with mean μ and standard deviation σ
  • 68% within 1σ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ
  • Standardize: z = (x − μ)/σ, then use tables or Q(z)
  • Thermal noise, measurement error, manufacturing spread
  (pen marks: 5 )
  beat0:
     0: The most important distribution in engineering is the Gaussian, or normal, distribution: the bell curve, set by its mean and its standard deviation.
  beat1:
     0: Memorize the 68, 95, 99.7 rule.
     1: About sixty eight percent of values fall within one sigma of the mean, ninety five percent within two sigma, and ninety nine point seven percent within three.
  beat2:
     0: For any other question, standardize: subtract the mean and divide by sigma to get z, then look up the probability in a table or with the Q function, which gives the tail area beyond z.
  beat3:
     0: Example: one kilohm resistors with a one percent tolerance, modeled with sigma of ten ohms.
     1: Ninety five percent fall between nine hundred eighty and one thousand twenty ohms.
     2: Thermal noise in every resistor, measurement errors, and variations in transistor thresholds across a wafer are all approximately Gaussian, which is why this curve is everywhere.

[s11] V  Why the bell curve is everywhere: the Central Limit Theorem
  img: a:galton / fig:clt
  • Add many independent random effects…
  • …and the sum becomes Gaussian, whatever the originals were
  • Sum of 12 uniforms ≈ Gaussian already
  • Noise = sum of countless tiny random kicks
  (pen marks: 3 )
  beat0:
     0: Why does the Gaussian show up everywhere?
     1: Look at the Galton board.
     2: Each ball's final position is the sum of many small random left or right bounces.
     3: And the pile is a bell curve.
  beat1:
     0: The central limit theorem says that the sum of many independent random effects is approximately Gaussian, no matter what each individual effect's distribution looks like.
     1: On the left, a single uniform random number is flat.
     2: Add two and you get a triangle.
     3: Add twelve, and it's already almost indistinguishable from a bell curve.
     4: Electrical noise is the combined effect of enormous numbers of electrons jiggling randomly, so it's Gaussian.
     5: Measurement error is the sum of many small disturbances, so it's Gaussian too.

[s12] V  Reliability: the exponential distribution
  img: a:probe_card / fig:exponential
  • Constant failure rate λ → lifetime is exponential
  • Mean time to failure MTTF = 1/λ
  • P(survive past t) = e^(−λt)
  • Memoryless — plus “burn-in” to weed out early failures
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: Reliability engineering asks how long things last.
     1: Chips are tested at the wafer level with probe cards like this one, and samples are stress tested to estimate failure rates.
     2: When a part fails at a constant rate, lambda, its lifetime follows the exponential distribution.
  beat1:
     0: The mean time to failure is one over lambda, and the probability of surviving past time t is e to the minus lambda t.
     1: Surprisingly, only about thirty seven percent of parts survive past their mean time to failure, and only thirteen and a half percent past twice that.
     2: The exponential is also memoryless: a part that has worked for a year is no more likely to fail next week than a new one.
     3: Real electronics also have a burst of early failures, called infant mortality, so manufacturers run new parts hot for a while, a process called burn in, to weed out the weak ones before shipping.

[s13] V  Two random variables: joint distributions and correlation
  img: fig:joint
  • Joint PDF f(x, y); marginals by integrating out
  • Independent ⇔ f(x, y) = f(x) f(y)
  • Covariance and correlation ρ ∈ [−1, 1]
  • Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2 Cov(X, Y)
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: With two random variables, we describe them together with a joint PDF.
     1: Integrate out one variable to get the other's own distribution, called the marginal.
  beat1:
     0: They're independent exactly when the joint PDF factors into the product of the marginals.
  beat2:
     0: Correlation measures how they move together, from minus one to plus one.
     1: On the left, rho is zero: a round cloud, no relationship.
     2: In the middle, rho of point seven: when one is high, the other tends to be high.
     3: On the right, rho of minus point nine: a strong inverse relationship.
     4: Remember that correlation measures linear relationship only, and it doesn't imply causation.
  beat3:
     0: And for sums: the variance of X plus Y is the sum of the variances plus twice the covariance.
     1: For independent noise sources, variances simply add, so two independent noise sources with equal power give root two times the noise voltage, not double.

[s14] V  Sums of random variables: convolution
  img: fig:conv
  • PDF of X + Y (independent) = f_X ∗ f_Y
  • Uniform + uniform = triangle
  • Same operation as LTI systems in Signals & Systems
  • Repeated convolution → Gaussian (CLT again)
  (pen marks: 4 )
  beat0:
     0: What's the distribution of the sum of two independent random variables?
     1: It's the convolution of their PDFs: slide one across the other, multiply and integrate.
  beat1:
     0: Two uniform variables on zero to one add up to a triangle on zero to two, the same shape you saw with the dice and the central limit theorem demo.
  beat2:
     0: Convolution is exactly the operation that describes linear time invariant systems in signals and systems, where the output is the input convolved with the impulse response.
     1: So you'll see it again very soon.
  beat3:
     0: Convolve many distributions together, and the result tends to a Gaussian.
     1: That's the central limit theorem from a different angle.

[s15] V  Engineering example: bit errors in noisy channels
  img: fig:snr
  • Send 0 → 0 V, 1 → A volts; noise is Gaussian (σ)
  • Receiver decides with a threshold at A/2
  • P(error) = Q(A / 2σ)
  • A = 4σ → 2.3% · A = 12σ → 1 in a billion
  (pen marks: 7 )
  beat0:
     0: Let's combine everything into the core problem of digital communication.
     1: A transmitter sends zero volts for a zero and A volts for a one.
     2: The channel adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation sigma.
     3: So a received zero is a bell curve centered at zero, and a received one is a bell curve centered at A.
  beat1:
     0: The receiver decides with a threshold halfway between, at A over two.
  beat2:
     0: An error happens when noise pushes a signal across the threshold, the red areas.
     1: The probability is Q of A over two sigma: the tail of a Gaussian beyond a distance of A over two.
  beat3:
     0: If A is four sigma, the error rate is Q of two, about two point three percent: terrible.
     1: Triple the signal to twelve sigma, and the error rate drops to about one in a billion.
     2: That's why signal to noise ratio is everything in communications, and why a few extra decibels of signal can cut error rates by orders of magnitude.
     3: Wi Fi, cellular, Ethernet and USB are all designed around exactly this calculation.
